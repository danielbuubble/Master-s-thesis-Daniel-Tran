\chapter{Methods}
\label{ch:methods}
This chapter describes the design and implementation of a system that simultaneously acquires \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} signals from a single muscle site, with galvanic isolation as the central design requirement.

Section~\ref{sec:design_specifications} states the system-level design specifications. Section~\ref{sec:system_architecture} introduces the top-level topology. Section~\ref{sec:hardware_development} details the analog hardware. Section~\ref{sec:equipment_software_tools} lists the equipment and software tools used. Section~\ref{sec:validation} describes the validation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:signal_processing} describes the signal-processing pipeline that produced the metrics reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_architecture}
%Here is the problem and our top-level solution
Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture} shows the system topology that supports simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} acquisition from a single muscle site. The architecture comprises two dedicated front-end units (one per modality), galvanic isolation stages between each front-end and the data acquisition unit, and a single \gls{daq} card connected to a PC. The galvanic isolation places each front-end in its own ground domain so that simultaneous acquisition does not introduce coupling between the two measurement circuits.

Each modality was equipped with its own set of surface electrodes placed over the target measurement site. The raw signals were processed within their respective front-end units, where they were amplified and filtered according to the bandwidth and dynamic range of each modality. After conditioning, the signals from each front-end passed through an isolated amplifier stage that provided galvanic isolation. On the system side of the isolation barrier, the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} channels were directed to separate analog input channels on the NI USB-6356. These channels were sampled simultaneously and transmitted to a PC for offline analysis.

The power architecture operated on the same principle. A single \SI{5}{\volt} supply fed two independent, isolated DC-DC converters, one for each front-end. This ensured that each front-end operated from its own isolated power supply and communicated with the data acquisition unit through its own signal-isolation stage. The combination of power and signal isolation enabled galvanic separation between the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} front-ends, minimizing ground-loop interference and crosstalk between the two modalities while still permitting simultaneous data acquisition on a shared device. The following sections describe the implementation of each component in the order they appear in the signal chain.

\section{Hardware Development}
\label{sec:hardware_development}
%Describe how I implemented it
The analog front-end is the signal-conditioning circuit between the electrodes and the galvanic isolation stage. The \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} front-ends each have their own signal chain, described in Sections~\ref{sec:EMG_frontend} and~\ref{sec:EDA_frontend}. Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation} describes the galvanic isolation stage that follows each front-end, and Section~\ref{sec:PSU} describes the power-supply architecture that feeds both chains.

The circuit was implemented on a custom four-layer \gls{pcb}, designed in KiCad and fabricated with help from ELAB at UiO.
\subsection{EMG Front-End}
\label{sec:EMG_frontend}
%Total gain = 733.3

%The component designators in the PCB layout differ from those in the schematic figures, as the schematic was redrawn for clarity.
The \gls{emg} front-end was inspired by the five-stage architecture proposed by \textcite{yang_detection_2020}. The instrumentation amplifier, \SI{50}{\hertz} notch, and voltage-lifting stages use the same topologies as Yang's design with updated component values. The high-pass and low-pass filter stages were redesigned with different topologies, described in Sections~\ref{sec:EMG_hpf} and~\ref{sec:EMG_lpf}. From the electrodes, the signal passed through an instrumentation amplifier, a \SI{50}{\hertz} notch filter, a high-pass filter with an amplification stage, a Sallen-Key low-pass filter, and a voltage-lifting circuit. Each stage was powered by an isolated \SI{\pm 5}{\volt} dual rail from the TPS65133, described in Section~\ref{sec:EMG_power}. The resulting block diagram is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}.

\subsubsection{Instrumentation Amplifier}
\label{sec:ina828}
The first stage was an INA828 instrumentation amplifier (Texas Instruments). Its CMRR of \SI{110}{\decibel} satisfied the \SI{80}{\decibel} minimum recommended for controlled isometric sEMG recordings~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. Its input impedance of \SI{100}{\giga\ohm}~\cite{noauthor_ina828_nodate} far exceeded the \SIrange{1}{10}{\mega\ohm} range recommended for \gls{semg} amplifiers~\cite{konrad_abc_2006} and ensured negligible loading of the electrode-skin source. The gain was set by a single external resistor $R_G$ via the datasheet relation~\cite{noauthor_ina828_nodate}:

\begin{equation}
    G = 1 + \frac{\SI{50}{\kilo\ohm}}{R_G}.
    \label{eq:ina828-gain}
\end{equation}
With $R_G = \SI{1.28}{\kilo\ohm}$ connected between the $R_G$ pins, the gain was $G = 1 + 50/1.28 \approx \num{40}$.

\subsubsection{Analog Filter Chain}
After the instrumentation amplifier, the signal passed through three filter stages. The RC corner frequency used in the filter calculations below is given by:
\begin{equation}
    f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}.
    \label{eq:RC_fc}
\end{equation}

The non-inverting amplifier gain, set by the feedback resistor $R_f$ and the input resistor $R_i$, is given by:
\begin{equation}
    G = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_i}.
    \label{eq:noninv_gain}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{50\,Hz Notch Filter}
Power-line interference appears as a large, narrowband disturbance at \SI{50}{\hertz} in the European grid environment. The \gls{semg} signal contains useful spectral content on both sides of \SI{50}{\hertz}, so a notch filter was used rather than raising the high-pass cutoff.
A twin-T active notch topology was implemented, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}b, with the notch frequency set to \SI{50}{\hertz} by the passive network values. The active feedback path, implemented using an \gls{opamp}, increased the notch's quality factor beyond what a passive twin-T alone could achieve.
The quality factor was determined from the simulated frequency response (Figure~\ref{fig:EMG_LTSpice_bode}):
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{f_0}{f_\text{upper} - f_\text{lower}} 
    = \frac{\SI{50}{\hertz}}{\SI{55.2}{\hertz} - \SI{44.7}{\hertz}} 
    \approx 5
    \label{eq:notch_q}
\end{equation}
where $f_\text{upper}$ and $f_\text{lower}$ are the upper and lower \SI{-3}{\decibel} frequencies from the Bode plot.


\paragraph{High-Pass Filter} %Gain = 11
\label{sec:EMG_hpf}
The high-pass stage consisted of a passive RC filter followed by a non-inverting amplifier stage. From Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}c, components $CG4 = \SI{1}{\micro\farad}$ and $RG6 = \SI{15}{\kilo\ohm}$ set the cutoff frequency to $f_c = 1/(2\pi \cdot \SI{15}{\kilo\ohm} \cdot \SI{1}{\micro\farad}) \approx \SI{10.6}{\hertz}$ via Equation~\ref{eq:RC_fc}. The resistors $RG7 = \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $RG8 = \SI{100}{\kilo\ohm}$ set the gain to $G = 1 + R_{G8}/R_{G7} = 1 + 100/10 = 11$ via Equation~\ref{eq:noninv_gain}.

The International Society for Electrophysiology and Kinesiology (ISEK) recommended a \SI{10}{\hertz} high-pass cutoff for sEMG recordings under controlled conditions, and \SI{20}{\hertz} for dynamic movements where motion artifacts were more pronounced~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. The protocol (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) used isometric contractions, in which the muscle generates sustained force against an external resistance. The \SI{10}{\hertz} cutoff was selected to preserve the low-frequency spectral content associated with motor unit firing patterns~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}.


\paragraph{Low-Pass Filter} %Gain = 5/3
\label{sec:EMG_lpf}
The final stage was a second-order Sallen-Key low-pass filter. When both resistors and both capacitors are equal, the cutoff frequency reduces to the same expression as a passive RC circuit, given by Equation~\ref{eq:RC_fc}~\cite{mancini_ron_op_2002}. From Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}d, components $CG5 = CG6 = \SI{0.1}{\micro\farad}$ and $RG9 = RG10 = \SI{3.16}{\kilo\ohm}$ gave a calculated cutoff $f_c = 1/(2\pi \cdot \SI{3.16}{\kilo\ohm} \cdot \SI{0.1}{\micro\farad}) \approx \SI{504}{\hertz}$ via Equation~\ref{eq:RC_fc}, near the \SI{500}{\hertz} upper bandwidth limit of the \gls{semg} spectrum~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. From Equation~\ref{eq:noninv_gain}, the resistors $RG11 = \SI{15}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $RG12 = \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}$ set the gain to $G = 1 + R_{G12}/R_{G11} = 1 + 10/15 \approx \num{1.67}$. The simulated cutoff was \SI{530}{\hertz}, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:EMG_LTSpice_bode}.

\subsubsection{Voltage Lifting Circuit} 
\label{sec:EMG_voltage_lifter}
The voltage-lifting stage preserves compatibility with single-rail \glspl{adc} that would otherwise require restructuring the front-end. The bipolar \gls{semg} output is centered at a positive midpoint of \SI{1.237}{\volt}, set by an LM285 precision voltage reference, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}e. The NI USB-6356 \gls{daq} used in this thesis accepts bipolar input directly, so the centering is not required for the validation but maintains design portability.

The output \texttt{EMG\_VLC} was passed to the galvanic isolation stage, described in Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}.

\subsubsection{Total Chain Gain}
\label{sec:EMG_total_gain}
The total voltage gain was the product of the instrumentation amplifier gain and the filter chain gain:
\begin{equation}
    G_\text{total} = G_\text{INA828} \times G_\text{filters}
    = G_\text{INA828} \times (G_\text{HPF} \times G_\text{LPF}) = \num{40} \cdot \num{11} \cdot \num{1.67} \approx \num{733.3}
    \label{eq:total-gain}
\end{equation}
The front-end output reached approximately \SI{220}{\milli\volt} at the maximum \gls{semg} amplitude of \SI{300}{\micro\volt} (Section~\ref{sec:sEMG_signal}), well within the \SI{\pm5}{\volt} input range of the \gls{daq} card. The Bode plot in Figure~\ref{fig:EMG_LTSpice_bode} showed the simulated frequency response of the complete front-end.

\subsection{EDA Front-End}
\label{sec:EDA_frontend}
The \gls{eda} front-end followed the design of \textcite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}, with all component values unchanged. The only difference was replacing the AD860x with the MAX44243AUD+, which was already used in the \gls{emg} front-end. Three stages made up the circuit: a virtual ground reference, a voltage-controlled current source, and a third-order low-pass filter. Power came from a \SI{3.3}{\volt} rail derived from the AMS1117-3.3, described in Section~\ref{sec:EDA_power}. The circuit was shown in Figure~\ref{fig:EDA-frontend-blocks}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/version_2_figures-EDA Building Block (cropped) (pdfresizer.com).pdf}
    \caption{EDA front-end building blocks. \textbf{(a)} Virtual ground. \textbf{(b)} Current source. \textbf{(c)} Low-pass filter.}
    \label{fig:EDA-frontend-blocks}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Virtual Ground}
Under single-supply operation, the \gls{opamp} output cannot swing below ground~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}. A mid-supply reference at \SI{1.65}{\volt} provides headroom for the \gls{eda} signal's tonic \gls{scl} baseline and the superimposed phasic \gls{scr}s that ride on it. A resistor divider ($R_{A1} = R_{A2} = \SI{100}{\kilo\ohm}$) split the \SI{3.3}{\volt} supply to give \SI{1.65}{\volt}, buffered by \gls{opamp} U3A to hold a low-impedance reference at the non-inverting input of U3B, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:EDA-frontend-blocks}a. $C_{A1}$ formed a low-pass filter with the divider impedance to keep supply noise off the reference node. $C_{b1}$, $C_{b2}$, and $C_b$ bypassed the \SI{3.3}{\volt} rail.

\subsubsection{Current Source}
\label{sec:EDA_current_source}
The reference resistor $R_{ref1}$ and the virtual ground voltage work together to determine the current injected into the skin~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}:

\begin{equation}
    I_{skin} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{V_{DD}}{R_{ref}}
    \label{eq:EDA_skin_current}
\end{equation}

With $R_{ref1} = \SI{825}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $V_{DD} = \SI{3.3}{\volt}$, the injected current was $\approx \SI{2}{\micro\ampere}$. For the electrode gel area used in this thesis, this corresponded to a current density well below the recommended limit of \SI{10}{\micro\ampere\per\centi\meter\squared}~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012}.

The skin resistance followed from the measured output voltage as:

\begin{equation}
    R_{skin} = \left(1 - \frac{V_{EDA}}{V_{DD}}\right) R_{ref}
    \label{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}
\end{equation}

where $V_{EDA}$ is the signal at the low-pass filter output. The Sallen-Key low-pass filter adds a gain of 2 (Section~\ref{sec:EDA_LPF}), giving $V_{EDA} = 2 V_{out}$. Substituting into the original expression from~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017} cancels the factor of two and gives Equation~\ref{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}.
Skin conductance was then obtained by taking the inverse of Equation~\ref{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}.


\subsubsection{Low-Pass Filter}\label{sec:EDA_LPF}
The low-pass filter limited the signal to the EDA bandwidth with a cutoff frequency of \SI{1.5}{\hertz}~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}. A second-order active Sallen-Key stage ($R_{A3}$, $R_{A5}$, $C_{A5}$, $C_{A6}$) with a Butterworth response was followed by a first-order passive RC low-pass stage ($R_{A7}$, $C_{A8}$), giving a third-order roll-off overall. The Sallen-Key feedback network ($R_{A4}$, $R_{A6}$) added a gain of \(\times 2\), expanding the output range to match the DAQ input better. The filter output is denoted by $V_{EDA}$ throughout this thesis. This cutoff imposed a lower bound of approximately \SI{0.67}{\second} on the \gls{scr} rise time observable through the filter.

The output $V_{EDA}$ was passed to the galvanic isolation stage, described in Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}.


\subsection{Implementation of Galvanic Isolation}
\label{sec:galvanic_isolation}
Without isolation, the \gls{emg} ground electrode (Section~\ref{sec:sEMG_electrodes}) and the \gls{eda} return electrode would share a common ground, creating a parallel current path through the body and establishing the conditions for a ground loop as described in Section~\ref{sec:ground_loops}. On the \gls{eda} side, this shared path would allow the excitation current to be diverted from the reference resistor, corrupting the conductance measurement. On the \gls{emg} side, the same path would couple the \gls{eda} excitation current into the high-gain \gls{emg} input, introducing a \gls{dc} offset and a current-injection artifact. Galvanic isolation resolved the problem by giving each front-end its own ground domain, breaking the shared current path. Both front-ends used the same isolation scheme, comprising power isolation and signal isolation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/version_2_figures-Galvanic Isolation (cropped) (pdfresizer.com).pdf}
    \caption{Building blocks of galvanic isolation. \textbf{(a)} Power isolation. \textbf{(b)} Signal isolation. \textbf{(c)} Differential amplifier.}
    \label{fig:isolation_blocks}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Architecture of Ground Domains}

The isolation process created three distinct ground domains, as represented in Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}: GND for the power supply unit (PSU) and data acquisition (DAQ) card, GND\_G for the \gls{emg} front-end, and GND\_A for the \gls{eda} front-end. Resistors \( R_{L1} = R_{L2} = \SI{10}{\mega\ohm} \) connected each isolated domain to GND to prevent floating of the isolated planes. The high resistance value ensured that minimal current flowed between the domains.

\subsubsection{Power Isolation}

Each front-end was powered by a dedicated NXE2S0505MC-R7 isolated DC-DC converter (\SI{5}{\volt} input, \SI{5}{\volt} output, \SI{2}{\watt}), as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}a. The output filter adhered to the recommended application circuit from the datasheet~\cite{noauthor_nxe2s0505mcisolated_nodate}, featuring a \SI{22}{\micro\henry} inductor and decoupling capacitors.

\subsubsection{Signal Isolation}

For signal isolation, the ISO224 reinforced-isolated amplifier was used, implemented according to the recommended application circuit in the datasheet~\cite{noauthor_iso224_nodate}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}b. Both front-ends operated from a \SI{5}{\volt} supply, ensuring that all signals remained well within the ISO224's allowable input range of \SI{\pm 12}{\volt}. The ISO224 had a fixed gain of $1/3$, set by the available device variant rather than by a specific design target. On the non-isolated side, the ISO224 produced a differential output centered on \( V_{DD2}/2 = \SI{2.5}{\volt} \). This common-mode bias is part of the device's output stage and allows bipolar signal swing on the unipolar \( V_{DD2} \) supply~\cite{noauthor_iso224_nodate}. A unity-gain differential amplifier (MAX44241AUK+T, using four matched \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm} resistors) converted this to a single-ended signal, effectively canceling the \SI{2.5}{\volt} offset, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}c.


The \gls{daq} (introduced in Section~\ref{sec:system_architecture}) input range was configured to \SI{\pm 5}{\volt}, chosen to provide sufficient headroom for the anticipated signals while optimizing ADC resolution. The validation of the isolated system is addressed in Section~\ref{sec:validation}.


\subsection{Power Supply}
\label{sec:PSU}
The power supply architecture maintained three distinct ground domains. The non-isolated domain (GND) hosted the USB-C input that supplied \SI{5}{\volt} to the circuit, and it connected to the \gls{daq} card on the system side of the isolation barrier. The isolated GND\_G domain hosted the \gls{emg} front-end together with its TPS65133 dual-rail converter. The isolated GND\_A domain hosted the \gls{eda} front-end together with its AMS1117-3.3 regulator. Each front-end derived its isolated rail from the USB-C \SI{5}{\volt} supply through the NXE2S0505MC-R7 DC-DC converters described in Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}. The \gls{daq} card was powered independently from the mains, with no shared power path with the circuit. The power supply building blocks are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}.

\subsubsection{USB-C Power Supply}
The \SI{5}{\volt} rail was derived from the VBUS pin of a USB-C receptacle, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}a. Two \SI{5.1}{\kilo\ohm} pull-down resistors on CC1 and CC2 identified the circuit as a power sink to the host, prompting it to supply \SI{5}{\volt} without requiring active power delivery negotiation~\cite{noauthor_usb_nodate}. The data lines and sideband use (SBU) pins were left unconnected, as the connector was used solely for power.

\subsubsection{EMG Power Supply}
\label{sec:EMG_power}
The \gls{emg} front-end required a dual-rail supply for the INA828 and signal-conditioning \glspl{opamp}. A TPS65133DPDR generated \SI{\pm 5}{\volt} from the isolated \SI{5}{\volt} input on GND\_G, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}b. The output filter adhered to the recommended application circuit from the datasheet~\cite{noauthor_tps65133_nodate}, with $L_{G1} = L_{G2} = \SI{4.7}{\micro\henry}$ and decoupling capacitors on both rails.

\subsubsection{EDA Power Supply}
\label{sec:EDA_power}
The \gls{eda} front-end operated on a single \SI{3.3}{\volt} rail. An AMS1117-3.3 regulator converted the isolated \SI{5}{\volt} from GND\_A to \SI{3.3}{\volt}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}c. An input capacitor $C_{A9} = \SI{10}{\micro\farad}$ and an output capacitor $C_{A10} = \SI{22}{\micro\farad}$ followed the recommended application circuit from the datasheet~\cite{evvo_ams1117_nodate}. This rail served as VDD for both the current source and the virtual ground divider in the \gls{eda} front-end.

\section{Validation Protocol}
\label{sec:validation}

The validation of the acquisition system proceeded across five recording sessions. A session is a discrete data-gathering recording during which the participant followed a fixed protocol on a single calendar day. Sessions 1 and 2 verified each front-end individually with the other circuit physically disconnected, isolating the per-circuit behavior from any cross-modal interaction. Sessions 3 to 5 acquired \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} simultaneously through the post-isolation channels, providing the data on which cross-modality and reproducibility were assessed. Section~\ref{sec:validation_Q} introduces the seven validation questions that structured the analysis.

\subsection{Validation Questions}
\label{sec:validation_Q}
The validation was organized around seven questions, denoted Q1 to Q7, that structure the analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:signal_processing}. The questions are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Q\arabic*}]
    \item What are the signal characteristics of the \gls{emg} front-end during EMG-only operation, and what are its limitations?
    \item What are the signal characteristics of the \gls{eda} front-end during EDA-only operation, and what are its limitations?
    \item How does the galvanic isolation stage affect signal amplitude, frequency content, waveform morphology, and added noise?
    \item How does digital post-filtering affect \gls{emg} signal quality at the pre- and post-isolation stages?
    \item How does simultaneous \gls{emg} acquisition affect the \gls{eda} measurement?
    \item How does simultaneous \gls{eda} acquisition affect the \gls{emg} measurement?
    \item How consistent are the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} measurements across repeated simultaneous recording sessions?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Participants}
The participant was one healthy adult (the author), right-handed, with no known neuromuscular or dermatological disorders affecting the hands. The recordings constituted a technical self-experiment conducted solely for circuit validation. Since the validation involved no clinical intervention and no participant outside the author, no formal ethics approval was sought.

\subsection{Acquisition Software}
\label{sec:acquisition_software}
A custom Python script, \texttt{daq\_gui\_multi\_validation.py}, handled data acquisition throughout all sessions. It interfaced with the NI USB-6356 via the \texttt{nidaqmx} library and sampled all active channels simultaneously at \SI{2}{\kilo\hertz}. Raw data were written to disk in TDMS format throughout each session. A real-time digital filter chain ran in parallel for visual monitoring and was excluded from the offline analysis.

\subsection{Electrode Placement and Skin Preparation}

\subsubsection{Skin Preparation}

Skin preparation was kept minimal to preserve the skin's electrical properties at the EDA sites. The same minimal protocol was applied at the EMG sites, despite the standard recommendation of alcohol cleaning and light abrasion for EMG recordings~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010, konrad_abc_2006}. Because the EMG and EDA electrodes shared the palm, a unified protocol was required, and protecting the EDA signal took priority. The participant washed both hands with soap and water and dried them with a paper towel. No lotion or skin product was applied on the morning of the recording. Jewelry was removed from the left hand and wrist.

\subsubsection{Electrode Placement}
\label{sec:electrode_placement}
This thesis targeted the biceps as the clinical deployment site, with \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} electrodes co-located on the same muscle. For technical validation, the left palm was used instead. Palmar \gls{eda} sites are reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:eda_electrode_sites}. The thenar eminence (at the muscular base of the thumb) carries voluntary thumb muscles that produce reliable \gls{semg} bursts on demand. The palm, therefore, provided both a known \gls{eda} reference signal and a cooperative \gls{emg} source. This allowed front-end performance to be benchmarked before future deployment at the biceps.

Three electrodes were used for EMG and two for EDA, with positions shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hand_electrode_placement}. Two different electrode types were used, one per modality: Ambu BlueSensor Q wet-gel electrodes for EMG, and Kendall H135SG solid-gel electrodes for EDA. The wet gel of the Ambu electrode provides low electrode-skin impedance favorable for EMG, while the solid gel of the Kendall electrode avoids gel migration into the stratum corneum that would otherwise bias the EDA conductance reading~\cite{tronstad_current_2022, martinsen_sources_2015}. 

For EMG, two active electrodes were placed over the thenar eminence in a bipolar configuration. They were aligned along the length of the thumb muscles with an edge-to-edge inter-electrode distance of \qty{10}{\milli\meter}\footnote{The recommended inter-electrode distance of \qty{20}{\milli\meter} for large muscle bellies~\cite{konrad_abc_2006} was reduced to \qty{10}{\milli\meter} to keep both electrodes within the thenar muscle belly, which is considerably smaller than the muscles on which the recommendation is based.}. The EMG ground electrode was placed on the back of the left wrist, at the position where a wristwatch would normally sit.

For EDA, two electrodes were placed on the hypothenar eminence of the left palm with a center-to-center distance of \qty{10}{\milli\meter}\footnote{Inter-electrode distances in this thesis follow the convention used in each literature tradition: edge-to-edge for EMG and center-to-center for EDA.}. This left the thenar site available for the EMG pair.

\subsection{Stimulation Instructions}

Cues are the participant-facing audiovisual prompts displayed at each scheduled time. Event markers are the corresponding TDMS timestamps recorded at each cue onset.

%Stimulation cue onset timestamps were saved as event markers in the TDMS file during acquisition.
\paragraph{EMG Stimulation}
The participant sat with the left forearm resting on the table, elbow flexed approximately \SI{90}{\degree} and the palm facing up. The fingers were relaxed in a neutral, slightly open position. 

An FSR400 force sensor was placed on top of the index finger before each block and remained in position throughout. At each EMG stimulus cue, the participant brought the thumb tip into contact with the index finger tip as quickly as possible and held the contraction for \SI{1}{\second}. A multimeter connected to the FSR400 provided real-time resistance feedback, and the participant adjusted force to maintain the sensor resistance at approximately \SI{5}{\kilo\ohm} during each contraction. Based on the FSR400 datasheet~\cite{inc_fsr_nodate}, \SI{5}{\kilo\ohm} corresponds to approximately \SI{1}{\newton} of applied force (100~gf). After \SI{1}{\second}, the participant relaxed the hand and returned to the neutral position.

\paragraph{EDA Stimulation}
At each \gls{eda} stimulus cue, the participant inhaled deeply over 2~seconds from normal breathing, then exhaled normally and relaxed. Simultaneous visual and auditory cues from the control software guided the timing of the deep breath.

\subsection{Recording Procedure}

All sessions followed the same structure: a 30-minute stabilization period during which the final 10 minutes were captured as the stabilization recording, followed by a 30-minute test run. Sessions 1 and 2 verified each circuit individually, with only one circuit connected at a time. Sessions 3 to 5 validated simultaneous operation with both circuits connected.

\subsubsection{Session 1: EMG Circuit Verification} \label{sec:session1}
Only the EMG circuit was connected. The EDA circuit was physically disconnected. Two channels were recorded simultaneously throughout:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pre-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
    \item Post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Electrodes were applied as described above (EMG electrodes only).
    \item The participant rested quietly for 30~minutes to allow the electrode-skin impedance to stabilize.
    \item During the final 10~minutes of this period, the \gls{emg} stabilization recording was captured on both channels.
    \item The 30-minute test run began. Every 15~seconds, a cue prompted the participant to perform one \gls{emg} stimulus of \SI{1}{\second}, followed by relaxation. This yielded 120 \gls{emg} stimuli.
\end{enumerate}

Post-session analysis of Session~1 data is described in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:comparative_analysis}.

\subsubsection{Session 2: EDA Circuit Verification} \label{sec:session2}
Only the \gls{eda} circuit was connected. The \gls{emg} circuit was physically disconnected. Two channels were recorded simultaneously throughout:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pre-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
    \item Post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Electrodes were applied as described above (EDA electrodes only).
    \item The participant rested quietly for 30~minutes to allow the electrode-skin impedance to stabilize.
    \item During the final 10~minutes of this period, the \gls{eda} stabilization recording was captured on both channels. The post-isolation stabilization recording served as the data source for the session-level \gls{eda} noise floor in the analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.
    \item The 30-minute test run began. Every 60~seconds, a cue prompted the participant to perform one deep-breath EDA stimulus. This yielded 30 EDA stimuli.
\end{enumerate}

Post-session analysis of Session~2 data is described in Sections~\ref{sec:eda_processing} and~\ref{sec:comparative_analysis}.

\subsubsection{Sessions 3 to 5: Simultaneous EMG and EDA} \label{sec:session3-5}
Both circuits were connected simultaneously. Unlike Sessions 1 and 2, which recorded pre- and post-isolation channels in parallel, only the post-isolation channels were recorded in Sessions 3 to 5:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \gls{emg} post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
    \item \gls{eda} post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
\end{itemize}
Each session consisted of a 30-minute stabilization period during which the final 10 minutes were captured as the stabilization recording, followed by a 30-minute test run.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} electrodes were applied as described above.
    \item The participant rested quietly for 30 minutes to allow the electrode-skin impedance to stabilize.
    \item During the final 10~minutes of this period, \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} stabilization recordings were captured while the participant remained still and breathed normally. The post-isolation \gls{eda} stabilization recording served as the data source for the session-level \gls{eda} noise floor in the analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}. The \gls{emg} stabilization recording was captured as a sanity check and was not used as a noise-floor source. \gls{emg} noise is estimated per epoch as described in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}.
    \item The 30-minute test run consisted of three 10-minute blocks:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Block 1: EDA-only (10 minutes)}\\ The participant remained seated with the left hand resting in the neutral position. Every 60 seconds, a cue prompted one deep-breath \gls{eda} stimulus. No voluntary hand or finger movement was performed. This block yielded 10 \gls{eda} stimuli.
        \item \textbf{Block 2: EMG-only (10 minutes)}\\ Every 15~seconds, a cue prompted one \gls{emg} stimulus of \SI{1}{\second}, followed by relaxation. No deep-breath \gls{eda} stimuli were performed. This block yielded 40 \gls{emg} stimuli.
        \item \textbf{Block 3: Combined EMG and EDA (10 minutes)}\\ Both stimulus types were performed according to the same timing as Blocks 1 and 2. Within each 60-second \gls{eda} cycle, the \gls{eda} cue fired first and four \gls{emg} cues followed at 15-second intervals, with the first \gls{emg} cue delayed by \SI{5}{\second} relative to the \gls{eda} cue. This block yielded 10 \gls{eda} stimuli and 40 \gls{emg} stimuli.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item In total, one session yielded 20 \gls{eda} stimuli (10 in EDA-only + 10 in combined) and 80 \gls{emg} stimuli (40 in EMG-only + 40 in combined).
    \item Sessions 3 to 5 were conducted on three separate days under the same conditions.
    \item The block order was fixed across Sessions 3 to 5: EDA-only first, EMG-only second, and Combined last.
\end{enumerate}

Stimulus cues were delivered through the graphical interface of the acquisition script (Section~\ref{sec:acquisition_software}). The event markers were used downstream to segment the continuous recordings into epochs for analysis (Section~\ref{sec:signal_processing}).

The validation protocol structures seven questions (Q1 to Q7) into individual front-end characterization, isolation-stage effects, simultaneous operation, and across-session reproducibility. Session~1 and Session~2 verified each front-end with the other circuit physically disconnected. Sessions 3 to 5 ran both circuits together under three block conditions: EDA-only, EMG-only, and Combined. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:signal_processing} against this protocol.

\subsection{Core Analysis Methods}
\label{sec:core_analysis}
% Section pipeline: overview → RMS → SNR → amplitude preservation → Pearson → PSD → handoff.
% Chapter role: defines the five signal-agnostic analysis methods reused in results and discussion.

% Section overview and forward pointer
%Five methods used: RMS, SNR, amplitude preservation, Pearson correlation, PSD - signal-agnostic definitions only
Five methods were used throughout this thesis to answer Q1 to Q7 (Section~\ref{sec:validation_Q}): \gls{rms}, \gls{snr}, amplitude preservation, Pearson correlation, and \gls{psd}. Each is defined here in modality-independent terms. Signal-specific implementations are given in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

% RMS: definition and EMG/EDA applicability
%RMS definition and equation - EMG only, state this explicitly, why not EDA
\gls{rms} quantified the average amplitude of a signal over a fixed time window. For a discrete signal $x[n]$ over $N$ samples, \gls{rms} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \text{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x[n]^2}
    \label{eq:rms}
\end{equation}
\gls{rms} is the standard amplitude measure in surface \gls{emg} literature~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}.

% SNR: definition and purpose
%SNR - general equation, S and N deferred
\gls{snr} quantified the amplitude of a desired signal relative to the background noise floor and indicated whether the stimulus-evoked signal was distinguishable from the noise floor:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{S}{N}\right)
    \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the signal amplitude and $N$ is the noise floor. The definitions of $S$ and $N$ differ between \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} and are given in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

% Amplitude preservation: isolation amplitude check
%Amplitude preservation - generic X, defined per signal downstream, used in Q1
Amplitude preservation quantified how well the isolation stage preserved signal amplitude across the ISO224. The ISO224 introduced a fixed gain of $1/3$ that was compensated in software before this metric was computed (Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}). A value of \SI{0}{\decibel} indicated perfect amplitude preservation:
\begin{equation}
    A_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{X_\text{post}}{X_\text{pre}}\right)
    \label{eq:amplitude_preservation}
\end{equation}
where $X$ is the contraction \gls{rms} for \gls{emg} (Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}) and the mean \gls{scr} amplitude for \gls{eda} (Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}).

% Pearson correlation: morphology preservation check
%Pearson correlation - morphology preservation, used in Q1
Pearson correlation quantified the linear similarity between two signals~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. For two discrete signals $x[n]$ and $y[n]$ of length $N$, the Pearson correlation coefficient\footnote{Kamen and Gabriel~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010} present the same expression as the cross-correlation function evaluated at zero lag, the form common in EMG signal-processing literature. The two are equivalent terms for the same quantity.} $r$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    r = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(x[n] - \bar{x}\right)\left(y[n] - \bar{y}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(x[n] - \bar{x}\right)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(y[n] - \bar{y}\right)^2}}
    \label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the sample means. The coefficient $r$ ranges from $-1$ to $+1$. A value of $r = +1$ indicates an identical waveform shape. Pearson $r$ was used in Q3 to verify that the isolation stage preserved signal morphology independently of the amplitude preservation check defined in Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation}.

% PSD: spectral analysis via Welch's method with per-segment detrending + section handoff
%PSD - Welch's method, per-segment detrending, pointers to signal-specific params
\gls{psd} characterized the front-end's frequency-domain behavior from the acquired biosignals by describing the distribution of signal power across frequency. A bench frequency-response sweep would require controlled input stimuli at the front-end's electrode interface, incompatible with the biosignal-driven validation used here. Full bench characterization is named as future work in Section~\ref{sec:disc-future}. The estimate used Welch's method~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}, which averaged periodograms of overlapping windowed segments to reduce spectral variance at the cost of frequency resolution. The segments overlapped by 50\%. Window length was set per signal in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

Each Welch segment was mean-subtracted before the Fourier transform to remove the DC pedestal that would otherwise dominate the lowest-frequency bin. The frequency bins are the discrete frequency intervals of the \gls{psd} output, with width set by the segment length and sampling rate. Three types of \gls{psd} were used: active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked. The chain noise is the noise contribution from the analog front-end and isolation stage measured when no biosignal is active. The active-window \gls{psd} captured the signal spectrum during stimulus response and was computed from the contraction window for \gls{emg} and the \gls{scr} detection window for \gls{eda}. The stabilization \gls{psd} captured the chain noise floor at rest and was computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} pre-session stabilization recording. The cue-masked \gls{psd} captured the chain noise floor during the test phase and was computed from the inter-cue windows of each recording.

The following two sections describe how each method was applied to the EMG and EDA signals, respectively. Scalar metrics are the single-value summaries reported per session or per condition, in contrast to the spectrum-valued \gls{psd} outputs presented separately as figures. Each scalar metric in Tables~\ref{tab:emg-metrics} and~\ref{tab:eda-metrics} is tagged with one of four classes. The state class names steady-state descriptors (noise floors and tonic levels) and the per-event class names single-stimulus responses (contraction \gls{rms} and \gls{scr} amplitude). The per-event aggregate class names counts or fractions pooled across events (detection rate) and the change-comparison class names relations between two values (amplitude preservation and \gls{snr}).
%A session-wide RMS would collapse the entire recording to a single power estimate and lose temporal resolution. Moving the window sample by sample yielded one RMS value per sample, preserving the time course of muscle activity.

%EMG is bipolar, so the signal was rectified before the window was applied. Without rectification, positive and negative values would partially cancel within each window, underestimating the true signal amplitude.
\subsection{EMG Signal Processing}
\label{sec:emg_processing}
%Pipeline order: epoch extraction → mean subtraction → scalar RMS → Q4 branch → contamination filter → noise floor → sliding window RMS → S and N definitions → PSD parameters

The following section describes, in pipeline order, how the core analysis methods were applied to the EMG signal.

\paragraph{Epoch Extraction}
%Epoch extraction (-0.5 to +3.5s), contraction window (0-1.2s), pre-cue (-0.5 to 0s), recovery for visualisation
The continuous \gls{emg} recording was segmented into epochs time-locked to each contraction cue. Each epoch spanned from \SI{0.5}{\second} before to \SI{3.5}{\second} after the cue onset, giving a \SI{4}{\second} window per contraction. Cue onset timestamps were extracted from the event markers stored in the TDMS file during acquisition. Two sub-windows were defined within each epoch: the pre-cue window from \SIrange{-0.5}{0}{\second} relative to the cue for DC offset estimation and noise floor computation, and the contraction window from \SIrange{0.0}{1.0}{\second} for signal amplitude estimation. The remainder of the epoch from \SI{1.0}{\second} to \SI{3.5}{\second} was included in figures to display the post-contraction return to baseline

%Per-epoch mean subtraction - pre-cue mean, not full epoch, reason
\paragraph{Mean Subtraction} 
After each epoch was extracted, the residual DC offset was removed by subtracting the pre-cue mean from all samples in that epoch. Using the pre-cue window rather than the full epoch mean avoided bias from the contraction, which would otherwise elevate the epoch mean and suppress both the baseline and contraction estimates. This step removed static DC contributions from the voltage lifting circuit (Section~\ref{sec:EMG_frontend}), electrode half-cell potentials, and amplifier input offsets.

The \gls{emg} signal oscillates symmetrically around zero, so the arithmetic mean is uninformative. Squaring each sample before averaging removes the sign and yields a direct amplitude measure.  

\paragraph{Scalar RMS}
%Scalar RMS - two values per epoch 
Two scalar RMS values were computed per epoch using Equation~\ref{eq:rms}. The pre-cue RMS was calculated across all samples within the pre-cue window and served as the per-epoch noise-floor estimate. The contraction RMS was calculated over all samples in the contraction window and served as the signal amplitude estimate.

\paragraph{Digital Filter Chain (Q4)}
%Q4 parallel path - filter chain after mean subtraction, zero-phase, notch BW
To address Q4, a three-stage digital filter chain was applied offline to the continuous signal before epoch extraction, creating a parallel path alongside the unfiltered signal. Per-epoch mean subtraction was applied to both paths after extraction. The chain consisted of a second-order Butterworth HPF at \SI{20}{\hertz}, an IIR notch at \SI{50}{\hertz} with $Q = 30$, and a fourth-order Butterworth LPF at \SI{500}{\hertz}. All stages used zero-phase filtering, applying each filter in the forward and backward directions to eliminate phase distortion. Zero-phase filtering was not available in the real-time analog chain because it requires access to future samples. The digital notch bandwidth was
\begin{equation}
    \Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q} = \frac{\SI{50}{\hertz}}{30} \approx \SI{1.67}{\hertz}
    \label{eq:digital_notch_bw}
\end{equation}
%This was narrower than the analog twin-T notch bandwidth of \SI{10.5}{\hertz} ($Q \approx 5$, Equation~\ref{eq:notch_q}), preserving more EMG spectral content around \SI{50}{\hertz}. 
This was narrower than the analog twin-T notch bandwidth of \SI{10.5}{\hertz} ($Q \approx 5$, Equation~\ref{eq:notch_q}). All subsequent processing steps were applied identically to the unfiltered and filtered signals.

%Contamination filter - empirical threshold, no citation
\paragraph{Contamination Filter} 
A contamination filter was applied to exclude epochs with elevated baseline activity before metric computation. An epoch was rejected if its pre-cue RMS exceeded twice the median pre-cue RMS across epochs in the same acquisition block ($\text{RMS}_\text{pre} > 2 \times \tilde{\text{RMS}}_\text{pre}$). This threshold was chosen empirically. Epochs above the threshold were assumed to contain pre-cue interference that would corrupt both the noise-floor and contraction-RMS estimates.
%Epochs above the threshold likely contained motion artifacts or other baseline interference that would corrupt both the noise floor and contraction RMS estimates.


\paragraph{Noise Floor}
%Noise floor - mean pre-cue RMS across clean epochs, why per-epoch
The noise floor was the mean pre-cue RMS across all epochs that passed the contamination filter. This per-epoch approach tracked signal conditions at each contraction rather than relying on a session-level noise floor that would not capture short-term changes in electrode contact or ambient noise.


\paragraph{Sliding-Window Envelope} 
%Sliding window RMS - linear envelope, visualization only, clean epochs only
Linear envelope detection was used to visualize the time course of muscle activation~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. The rectified EMG signal was processed with a \SI{100}{\milli\second} sliding RMS window, producing a smooth amplitude envelope that tracked muscle activity over time~\cite{konrad_abc_2006}. The \SI{100}{\milli\second} window balanced temporal resolution against stability of estimates. The sliding-window envelope was applied only to clean epochs and was not used in any metric computation.

\paragraph{SNR}
%S and N definitions for EMG SNR - computation moved to comparative analysis
For EMG, $S$ in Equation~\ref{eq:snr} was the mean contraction RMS across clean epochs, and $N$ was the noise floor as defined above.

\paragraph{PSD} 
%PSD parameters for EMG
EMG used the three \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:core_analysis}. The per-segment mean subtraction defined there removed the DC contribution of the voltage lifting circuit (Section~\ref{sec:EMG_frontend}) from the \gls{emg} spectrum. The active-window \gls{psd} was computed per epoch over the \SI{1}{\second} contraction window with a segment length of \SI{1}{\second}, and the per-epoch spectra were averaged across clean epochs. The stabilization \gls{psd} was computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} pre-session stabilization recording with a segment length of \SI{1}{\second}. The cue-masked \gls{psd} was computed from the inter-cue windows of the EMG recording with a segment length of \SI{1}{\second}. Each \gls{emg} cue was excluded from $-\SI{0.5}{\second}$ to $+\SI{3.5}{\second}$ relative to cue onset. 

\paragraph{Mains Harmonic Power}
%Mains harmonic power - quantify residual power-line interference
Mains harmonic power characterized residual power-line interference after the analog notch stage, computed from the active-window \gls{psd} (\autoref{sec:core_analysis}) at the \SI{50}{\hertz} fundamental and the \SI{100}{\hertz} and \SI{150}{\hertz} harmonics. For each harmonic, the power was integrated across a band of $\pm\Delta f$ around the center frequency, with $\Delta f$ from Equation~\ref{eq:digital_notch_bw}:
\begin{equation}
    P_\text{harmonic}(f_0) = \int_{f_0 - \Delta f}^{f_0 + \Delta f} \text{PSD}(f)\, df
     \label{eq:harmonic_power}
\end{equation}
where $f_0$ is the harmonic center frequency and $\Delta f = \SI{1.67}{\hertz}$. The total mains harmonic power was the sum across the three harmonics. The EDA signal required a different processing approach due to its DC-coupled nature and slower response dynamics, as described in the following section.

\subsection{EDA Signal Processing}
\label{sec:eda_processing}
%Pipeline order: epoch extraction → no filtering → no mean subtraction → SCL → noise floor → SCR detection → epoch averaging → SNR definition

The following section describes how the EDA signal was quantified. As described in Section~\ref{sec:EDA_signal}, the EDA signal consists of a tonic component, the skin conductance level (SCL), and a phasic component, the skin conductance response (SCR). The sampled voltage $V_\text{EDA}$ was converted to skin conductance using Equation~\ref{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}, with measurements expressed in \si{\micro\siemens}.

\paragraph{Epoch Extraction}
%Epoch extraction (-2 to +10s), baseline -2 to 0s, why 12s window
The EDA recording was segmented into epochs time-locked to each deep-inhalation cue, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}A. Each epoch spanned from \SI{2}{\second} before to \SI{10}{\second} after the cue onset, giving a \SI{12}{\second} window per trial. The pre-cue segment from \SIrange{-2}{0}{\second} was used for per-epoch \gls{scl} and noise-floor estimation. A longer pre-cue window than in the EMG case was used because EDA exhibits slower drift and requires more samples to obtain a stable tonic estimate. The post-cue window extended to \SI{10}{\second} to capture the full SCR morphology, including the recovery phase, which typically lasts several seconds after the response peak.

\paragraph{No Filtering}
%No digital filtering + reason
No digital filtering was applied to the EDA signal. The \SI{1.5}{\hertz} analog low-pass filter in the EDA front-end (Section~\ref{sec:EDA_frontend}) already bandlimits the signal to the relevant EDA frequency range. Additional digital filtering risked distorting the slow SCR waveform dynamics that carried the physiological information of interest.

\paragraph{No Mean Subtraction}
%No mean subtraction + reason
Mean subtraction was not applied to the EDA signal. Unlike EMG, EDA is DC-coupled, and its mean reflects the tonic skin conductance level. Removing the epoch mean would discard the SCL, which was itself a metric of interest.

%SCL - per-epoch pre-cue mean, why per-epoch, use in Q5
\paragraph{SCL} 
The SCL was computed as the mean conductance over the per-epoch pre-cue window, shown as the grey region in Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}B. This operationalization differs from the conventional session-level SCL estimate~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012}. The per-epoch form was chosen to provide a tonic estimate representative of signal conditions at the time of each stimulus, while capturing habituation-driven drift across the recording (the gradual decline in SCR responsiveness across repeated stimuli). The SCL also served as a between-condition comparator in Q5 to detect tonic contamination from simultaneous EMG operation.

\paragraph{Noise Floors} 
%Noise floor - two estimates, session-level circuit + per-epoch local, why not RMS
Two noise floor estimates were used for the EDA signal. The first was a session-level estimate computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} stabilization recording at the start of each session, during which the subject sat still with no stimulus. The recording was linearly detrended to remove residual SCL drift. The standard deviation of the detrended residual (population SD, dividing by $n$) was taken as the session-level noise floor $\sigma_\text{session}$. A separate $\sigma_\text{session}$ was computed for the pre- and post-isolation channels, since these correspond to different physical circuits with potentially different noise characteristics. This estimate represented the circuit noise floor of each EDA front-end stage under quiet conditions.

The second was a per-epoch estimate computed from the \SI{2}{\second} pre-cue window of each stimulus epoch. Each window was linearly detrended, and the standard deviation of the residual was computed. The per-epoch SDs (population SD, dividing by $n$) were averaged across all epochs in a block to give the local noise floor $\sigma_\text{local}$. This estimate represented the noise conditions immediately preceding each SCR, including circuit noise and non-specific activity. 

\gls{rms} was not used for either estimate. \gls{eda} is DC-coupled, and an \gls{rms} over the stabilization recording is dominated by the \gls{scl} mean and carries no noise-floor information.

%SCR detection - Zangróniz criteria, onset, 250ms smoothing, unsmoothed amplitude, dominant response
\paragraph{SCR Detection Criteria} 
SCR detection followed the criteria described by~\textcite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}. A response was considered valid if its amplitude exceeded \SI{0.05}{\micro\siemens} and its onset fell within the \SIrange{1}{6}{\second} post-cue detection window shown as the green region in Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}B. The \gls{scr} onset was identified as the conductance minimum within \SIrange{1}{3.5}{\second} post-cue, or up to the peak location if the peak fell before \SI{3.5}{\second}. Restricting the onset to this sub-window enforced physiologically plausible stimulus-response latency. The onset search used the smoothed signal to match the peak-detection procedure described below.

\paragraph{Peak Detection} 
The peak was permitted anywhere within the full \SIrange{1}{6}{\second} detection window. To reduce bias from noise on the peak location, the signal was smoothed with a \SI{250}{\milli\second} moving average before identifying the sample of maximum amplitude in the search window. The window suppressed noise spikes that would otherwise shift the peak location away from the physiological SCR peak without smearing the SCR itself. The SCR rise time is typically several seconds. The window length was chosen empirically.

\paragraph{Amplitude Reading} 
The SCR amplitude was read from the smoothed signal at the detected peak index (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}B). For consistency with the peak-detection procedure, the amplitude was read from the smoothed signal rather than the unsmoothed one. The same smoothing was applied to the pre-isolation signal to keep the comparison symmetrical. The SCR amplitude was computed as the peak-to-onset conductance difference. Where multiple threshold crossings occurred within a single epoch, only the dominant response (defined as the threshold crossing with the largest peak-to-onset amplitude within the detection window) was kept.

\paragraph{Epoch Averaging}
%Epoch averaging - noise suppression argument for post-isolation signal
The SCR amplitude reported for each block was the mean across all detected epochs within that block. Averaging across stimulus-locked epochs reduced the contribution of stimulus-uncorrelated noise sources, since such noise cancels in the mean while the physiological response does not.

%Averaging across stimulus-locked epochs reduced the noise contribution from the isolation stage, since broadband noise is uncorrelated with the stimulus and cancels in the mean, while the physiological response does not.

\paragraph{SNR}
%EDA SNR - two definitions, circuit and local, diagnostic meaning
Two EDA SNR values were computed per condition using the noise floors defined above. The circuit SNR used the session-level noise floor as the denominator:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_\text{EDA,circuit} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{A_\text{SCR}}{\sigma_\text{session}}\right)
    \label{eq:eda_snr_circuit}
\end{equation}
The local SNR used the per-epoch noise floor:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_\text{EDA,local} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{A_\text{SCR}}{\sigma_\text{local}}\right)
    \label{eq:eda_snr_local}
\end{equation}
In both equations, $A_\text{SCR}$ was the mean SCR amplitude across detected epochs within the condition.

\paragraph{PSD}
EDA used the three \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:core_analysis}. The active-window \gls{psd} was computed per epoch over the \SIrange{1}{6}{\second} \gls{scr} detection window with a segment length of \SI{5}{\second}, and the per-epoch spectra were averaged across all epochs. The stabilization \gls{psd} was computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} pre-session stabilization recording with a segment length of \SI{10}{\second}. The cue-masked \gls{psd} was computed from the inter-cue windows of the EDA recording with a segment length of \SI{10}{\second}. Each \gls{eda} cue was excluded from $-\SI{2.0}{\second}$ to $+\SI{10.0}{\second}$ relative to cue onset. The \SI{10}{\second} segment gave a frequency resolution of \SI{0.1}{\hertz}.

\subsubsection{Q3 - Isolation Stage Effect (EMG and EDA)}
\label{sec:q3_isolation_stage}

Q3 evaluated the effect of the galvanic isolation stage on the EMG and EDA signals across amplitude, frequency content, and waveform morphology. Data from Session~1 were used for the EMG comparison and data from Session~2 for the EDA comparison. All Q3 metrics were computed pre- and post-isolation per modality.

For \gls{emg}, amplitude preservation across the isolation barrier was computed using Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation} with $X$ as the mean contraction RMS across clean epochs. SNR was computed pre- and post-isolation using the mean pre-cue RMS as the noise floor to test whether the isolator degraded the \gls{emg} signal. Pearson $r$ was computed per epoch between the gain-corrected pre- and post-isolation signals over the full epoch window from \SIrange{-0.5}{3.5}{\second} using Equation~\ref{eq:pearson} to test whether the isolation stage preserved waveform morphology. The mean and within-block SD of Pearson~$r$ were reported across clean epochs. The active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing} were computed for the pre- and post-isolation \gls{emg} channels of Session~1 to compare the two channels.

For \gls{eda}, amplitude preservation across the isolation barrier was computed using Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation} with $X$ as the mean SCR amplitude across detected epochs. The circuit SNR, local SNR, and SCR detection rate were compared pre- and post-isolation using Equations~\ref{eq:eda_snr_circuit} and~\ref{eq:eda_snr_local} to test whether the isolation stage suppressed EDA phasic activity beyond the compensated gain. Pearson $r$ was computed per epoch between the gain-corrected pre- and post-isolation signals over the full epoch window from \SIrange{-2}{10}{\second} using Equation~\ref{eq:pearson} to test whether the isolation stage preserved waveform morphology. The mean, within-block SD, and median of Pearson~$r$ were reported across all epochs. The active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing} were computed for the pre- and post-isolation \gls{eda} channels of Session~2 to compare the two channels.

\subsubsection{Q4 - Digital Filtering Effect (EMG Only)}
\label{sec:q4_digital_filtering}

\paragraph{Signal Derivation} 
Q4 evaluated how offline digital filtering affected EMG signal quality at both the pre- and post-isolation stages. Four signals were derived from the two raw channels recorded in Session~1: pre-isolation raw, pre-isolation filtered, post-isolation raw, and post-isolation filtered. The filtered conditions used the digital filter chain defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}.

\paragraph{Noise Floor, RMS, and SNR} 
The noise floor and SNR were computed for each of the four signals using the mean pre-cue RMS across clean epochs as the noise floor and contraction RMS as the signal amplitude, following Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}.

\paragraph{Notch Depth and Harmonic Power} 
The notch depth at \SI{50}{\hertz} was computed for each filter chain (pre- and post-isolation) using
\begin{equation}
    D_\text{notch}(f_0) = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_\text{raw}(f_0)}{P_\text{filtered}(f_0)}\right)
    \label{eq:notch_depth}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the mains harmonic power at the harmonic centered on $f_0$ from Equation~\ref{eq:harmonic_power}, evaluated at $f_0 = \SI{50}{\hertz}$. This yielded two notch-depth values, one for the pre-isolation chain and one for the post-isolation chain. The mains harmonic power was computed at \SI{50}{\hertz}, \SI{100}{\hertz}, and \SI{150}{\hertz} using Equation~\ref{eq:harmonic_power} to quantify the per-harmonic suppression achieved by each filter chain. SNR, notch depth, and harmonic power comparisons between filtered and raw signals were used to quantify the signal quality recoverable through digital filtering.

\paragraph{PSD}
The active-window and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing} were computed and compared across the four conditions.

\paragraph{Single-Epoch Visualization}
A single clean epoch was also shown, without envelope detection, to visualize the filter's effect on the raw EMG waveform.

\subsubsection{Q5 - EMG Effect on EDA}
\label{sec:q5_emg_effect_on_eda}

Q5 evaluated whether simultaneous \gls{emg} operation contaminated the \gls{eda} signal. Three conditions were compared: the EDA-only reference from Session~2, the EDA-only block from Sessions 3 to 5, and the Combined block from Sessions 3 to 5.

The \gls{scl} was computed as the per-epoch pre-cue mean and compared across all three conditions to detect any tonic offset introduced by simultaneous \gls{emg} operation, following Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}. The \gls{scl} shift is a change-comparison metric: the per-session difference between Combined and \gls{eda}-only mean \gls{scl}, reported across Sessions 3 to 5 as the across-session mean and cross-session SD ($n = 3$). \gls{scr} amplitude, detection rate, circuit \gls{snr}, and local \gls{snr} were computed for each condition using Equations~\ref{eq:eda_snr_circuit} and~\ref{eq:eda_snr_local}, to test whether the galvanic isolation stage prevents \gls{emg} interference from reaching the \gls{scr}.

The active-window and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing} were computed for the Session~2 reference, the EDA-only block, and the Combined block to compare the three conditions. Active-window \gls{psd}s were pooled across Sessions 3 to 5 per condition, and cue-masked \gls{psd}s were averaged across Sessions 3 to 5 per condition.

\subsubsection{Q7 - Reproducibility}
\label{sec:q7_reproducibility}

Q7 evaluated the consistency of simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} measurements across repeated sessions. Data from Sessions 3 to 5 were used, with all metrics computed per session and reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation across the three sessions.

For \gls{emg}, the noise floor, contraction RMS, and SNR were reported separately for the EMG-only and Combined blocks. Cross-session SDs were used to assess front-end stability during repeated simultaneous operation in the presence of known sources of variability, such as electrode placement and skin preparation.

For EDA, the SCL, SCR amplitude, detection rate, circuit SNR, and local SNR were reported separately for the EDA-only and Combined blocks. The per-block SCL split preserved the within-session habituation drift described in Section~\ref{sec:EDA_signal}.
