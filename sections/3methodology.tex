\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_architecture}
%Here is the problem and our top-level solution
This section describes the system designed to measure \gls{emg} simultaneously and \gls{eda} at the same muscle site. The main hardware components are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture} and included two dedicated front-end units, one per modality, galvanic isolation stages, and a common data acquisition unit connected to a PC.

Each modality was equipped with its own set of surface electrodes placed over the target measurement site. The raw signals were processed within their respective front-end units, where they were amplified and filtered according to the bandwidth and dynamic range of each modality. After conditioning, the signals from each front-end passed through an isolated amplifier stage that provided galvanic isolation. On the system side of the isolation barrier, the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} channels were directed to separate analog input channels on the NI USB-6356. These channels were sampled simultaneously and transmitted to a PC for offline analysis.

The power architecture operated on the same principle. A single \SI{5}{\volt} supply fed two independent, isolated DC-DC converters, one for each front-end. This ensured that each front-end operated from its own isolated power supply and communicated with the data acquisition unit through its own signal-isolation stage. The combination of power and signal isolation enabled galvanic separation between the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} front-ends, minimizing ground-loop interference and crosstalk between the two modalities while still permitting simultaneous data acquisition on a shared device. The following sections describe the implementation of each component in the order they appear in the signal chain.

\section{Hardware Development}
%Describe how I implemented it
This section describes the implementation of the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} front-ends, the galvanic-isolation stage, and the power-supply architecture. In this thesis, the analog front-end is defined as the signal conditioning circuitry that runs from the measurement electrodes up to, but not including, the galvanic isolation stage.

The circuit was implemented on a custom four-layer \gls{pcb}, designed in KiCad and fabricated with help from ELAB at UiO.

\subsection{EMG Front-End}
\label{sec:EMG_frontend}
%Total gain = 733.3

%The component designators in the PCB layout differ from those in the schematic figures, as the schematic was redrawn for clarity.
The \gls{emg} front-end was inspired by the five-stage architecture proposed by \textcite{yang_detection_2020}. The instrumentation amplifier, \SI{50}{\hertz} notch, and voltage-lifting stages use the same topologies as Yang's design with updated component values, while the high-pass and low-pass filter stages were redesigned with different topologies. From the electrodes, the signal passed through an instrumentation amplifier, a \SI{50}{\hertz} notch filter, a high-pass filter with an amplification stage, a Sallen-Key low-pass filter, and a voltage-lifting circuit. Each stage was powered by an isolated \SI{\pm 5}{\volt} dual rail from the TPS65133, described in Section~\ref{sec:EMG_power}. The resulting block diagram is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}.

%As mentioned in \ref{sec:sEMG_signal} the useful frequency contents spans from \SIrange{10}{500}{\hertz}, with amplitude from active contraction of the muscle at \SIrange{60}{300}{\micro\volt}.

\subsubsection{Instrumentation Amplifier}
\label{sec:ina828}
The first stage was an INA828 instrumentation amplifier (Texas Instruments). Its CMRR of \SI{110}{\decibel} satisfied the \SI{80}{\decibel} minimum recommended for controlled isometric sEMG recordings~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. Gain was set to approximately \num{40} by connecting a \SI{1.28}{\kilo\ohm} resistor between the $R_G$ pins, following the relation given in the component datasheet~\cite{noauthor_ina828_nodate}:
\begin{equation}
    G = 1 + \frac{\SI{50}{\kilo\ohm}}{R_G}.
    \label{eq:ina828-gain}
\end{equation} 

\subsubsection{Analog Filter Chain}
After the instrumentation amplifier, the signal passed through three filter stages. The RC corner frequency used in the filter calculations below is given by:
\begin{equation}
    f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}.
    \label{eq:RC_fc}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{50\,Hz Notch Filter}
Power-line interference appears as a large, narrowband disturbance at \SI{50}{\hertz} in the European grid environment. The \gls{semg} signal contains useful spectral content on both sides of \SI{50}{\hertz}, so a notch filter was used rather than raising the high-pass cutoff.
A twin-T active notch topology was implemented, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}b, with the notch frequency set to \SI{50}{\hertz} by the passive network values. The active feedback path, implemented using an operational amplifier, increased the notch's quality factor beyond what a passive twin-T alone could achieve.
The quality factor was determined from the simulated frequency response (Figure~\ref{fig:EMG_LTSpice_bode}):
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{f_0}{f_\text{upper} - f_\text{lower}} 
    = \frac{\SI{50}{\hertz}}{\SI{55.2}{\hertz} - \SI{44.7}{\hertz}} 
    \approx 5
    \label{eq:notch_q}
\end{equation}
where $f_\text{upper}$ and $f_\text{lower}$ are the upper and lower \SI{-3}{\decibel} frequencies from the Bode plot.

\paragraph{High-Pass Filter} %Gain = 11
\label{sec:EMG_hpf}
The high-pass stage consisted of a passive RC filter followed by a non-inverting operational amplifier. From Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}c, components $CG4 = \SI{1}{\micro\farad}$ and $RG6 = \SI{15}{\kilo\ohm}$ set the cutoff frequency to $f_c = \SI{10}{\hertz}$ using Eq.~\ref{eq:RC_fc}, and components $RG7 = \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $RG8 = \SI{100}{\kilo\ohm}$ set the amplifier gain to \num{11}.
The International Society for Electrophysiology and Kinesiology (ISEK) recommended a \SI{10}{\hertz} high-pass cutoff for sEMG recordings under controlled conditions, and \SI{20}{\hertz} for dynamic movements where motion artifacts were more pronounced~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. Since the protocol employed isometric contractions, \SI{10}{\hertz} was selected to preserve the low-frequency spectral content associated with motor unit firing patterns~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}

\paragraph{Low-Pass Filter} %Gain = 5/3
\label{sec:EMG_lpf}
The final stage was a second-order Sallen-Key low-pass filter. When both resistors and both capacitors are equal, the cutoff frequency reduces to the same expression as a passive RC circuit, given by Equation~\ref{eq:RC_fc}. From Figure~\ref{fig:emg-frontend-blocks}d, components $CG5 = CG6 = \SI{0.1}{\micro\farad}$ and $RG9 = RG10 = \SI{3.16}{\kilo\ohm}$ gave a calculated cutoff of $f_c \approx \SI{500}{\hertz}$, the upper bandwidth limit of the \gls{semg} spectrum~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. The filter gain was set to \num{1.67} by the feedback resistors $RG11 = \SI{15}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $RG12 = \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm}$, following $G = 1 + R_{G12}/R_{G11}$. The simulated cutoff was \SI{530}{\hertz}, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:EMG_LTSpice_bode}.


\subsubsection{Voltage Lifting Circuit} 
\label{sec:EMG_voltage_lifter}
A voltage-lifting circuit offset the \gls{emg} output to a \SI{1.237}{\volt} midpoint, set by an LM285 precision voltage reference. The biphasic \gls{semg} waveform was thereby centered within the \gls{daq} input range, keeping both signal rails clear of clipping.

The output \texttt{EMG\_VLC} was passed to the galvanic isolation stage, described in Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}.

\subsubsection{Total Chain Gain}
\label{sec:EMG_total_gain}
The total voltage gain was the product of the instrumentation amplifier gain and the filter chain gain:
\begin{equation}
    G_\text{total} = G_\text{INA828} \times G_\text{filters}
    = G_\text{INA828} \times (G_\text{HPF} \times G_\text{LPF}) = \num{40} \times \num{11} \times \num{1.67} \approx \num{733.3}.
    \label{eq:total-gain}
\end{equation}
At the maximum \gls{semg} amplitude of \SI{300}{\micro\volt}, the front-end output reached approximately \SI{220}{\milli\volt}, well within the \SI{\pm5}{\volt} input range of the \gls{daq} card. The Bode plot in Figure~\ref{fig:EMG_LTSpice_bode} showed the simulated frequency response of the complete front-end.

\subsection{EDA Front-End}
\label{sec:EDA_frontend}
The \gls{eda} front-end followed the design of \textcite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}, with all component values unchanged. The only difference was replacing the AD860x with the MAX44243AUD+, which was already used in the \gls{emg} front-end. Three stages made up the circuit: a virtual ground reference, a voltage-controlled current source, and a third-order low-pass filter. Power came from a \SI{3.3}{\volt} rail derived from the AMS1117-3.3, described in Section~\ref{sec:EDA_power}. The circuit was shown in Figure~\ref{fig:EDA-frontend-blocks}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/version_2_figures-EDA Building Block (cropped) (pdfresizer.com).pdf}
    \caption{EDA front-end building blocks. \textbf{(a)} Virtual ground. \textbf{(b)} Current source. \textbf{(c)} Low-pass filter.}
    \label{fig:EDA-frontend-blocks}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Virtual Ground}
In a single-supply configuration, the op-amp has no negative rail, so a stable mid-supply reference is needed for the current source to operate correctly~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}. A resistor divider ($R_{A1} = R_{A2} = \SI{100}{\kilo\ohm}$) split the \SI{3.3}{\volt} supply to give \SI{1.65}{\volt}, buffered by op-amp U3A to hold a low-impedance reference at the non-inverting input of U3B, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:EDA-frontend-blocks}a. $C_{A1}$ formed a low-pass filter with the divider impedance to keep supply noise off the reference node. $C_{b1}$, $C_{b2}$, and $C_b$ bypassed the \SI{3.3}{\volt} rail.

\subsubsection{Current Source}
\label{sec:EDA_current_source}
The reference resistor $R_{ref1}$ and the virtual ground voltage work together to determine the current injected into the skin~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}:

\begin{equation}
    I_{skin} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{V_{DD}}{R_{ref}}
    \label{eq:EDA_skin_current}
\end{equation}

With $R_{ref1} = \SI{825}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $V_{DD} = \SI{3.3}{\volt}$, the injected current was $\approx \SI{2}{\micro\ampere}$. For the electrode gel area used in this thesis, this corresponded to a current density well below the recommended limit of \SI{10}{\micro\ampere\per\centi\meter\squared}~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012}.

The skin resistance followed from the measured output voltage as:

\begin{equation}
    R_{skin} = \left(1 - \frac{V_{EDA}}{V_{DD}}\right) R_{ref}
    \label{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}
\end{equation}

where $V_{EDA}$ is the signal at the low-pass filter output. Since $V_{EDA} = 2V_{out}$, substituting into the original expression from~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017} cancels the factor of two, giving Equation~\ref{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}.
Skin conductance was then obtained by taking the inverse of Equation~\ref{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}.

\subsubsection{Low-Pass Filter}
The low-pass filter limited the signal to the EDA bandwidth with a cutoff frequency of \SI{1.5}{\hertz}~\cite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}. A second-order active Sallen-Key stage ($R_{A3}$, $R_{A5}$, $C_{A5}$, $C_{A6}$) with a Butterworth response was followed by a first-order passive RC stage ($R_{A7}$, $C_{A8}$), giving a third-order roll-off overall. The Sallen-Key feedback network ($R_{A4}$, $R_{A6}$) added a gain of \(\times 2\), expanding the output range to match the DAQ input better. The filter output is denoted by $V_{EDA}$ throughout this thesis.

The output $V_{EDA}$ was passed to the galvanic isolation stage, described in Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}.


\subsection{Implementation of Galvanic Isolation}
\label{sec:galvanic_isolation}
Without isolation, the \gls{emg} ground electrode (Section~\ref{sec:sEMG_electrodes}) and the \gls{eda} return electrode would share a common ground, creating a parallel current path through the body and establishing the conditions for a ground loop as described in Section~\ref{sec:ground_loops}. This shared path would allow the \gls{eda} excitation current to be diverted from the reference resistor, corrupting the conductance measurement. Galvanic isolation resolved the problem by giving each front-end its own ground domain, breaking the shared current path. Both front-ends used the same isolation scheme, comprising power isolation and signal isolation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/version_2_figures-Galvanic Isolation (cropped) (pdfresizer.com).pdf}
    \caption{Building blocks of galvanic isolation. \textbf{(a)} Power isolation. \textbf{(b)} Signal isolation. \textbf{(c)} Differential amplifier.}
    \label{fig:isolation_blocks}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Architecture of Ground Domains}

The isolation process created three distinct ground domains, as represented in Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture}: GND for the power supply unit (PSU) and data acquisition (DAQ) card, GND\_G for the \gls{emg} front-end, and GND\_A for the \gls{eda} front-end. Resistors \( R_{L1} = R_{L2} = \SI{10}{\mega\ohm} \) connected each isolated domain to GND to prevent floating of the isolated planes. The high resistance value ensured that minimal current flowed between the domains.

\subsubsection{Power Isolation}

Each front-end was powered by a dedicated NXE2S0505MC-R7 isolated DC-DC converter (\SI{5}{\volt} input, \SI{5}{\volt} output, \SI{2}{\watt}), as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}a. The output filter adhered to the recommended application circuit from the datasheet~\cite{noauthor_nxe2s0505mcisolated_nodate}, featuring a \SI{22}{\micro\henry} inductor and decoupling capacitors.

\subsubsection{Signal Isolation}

For signal isolation, the ISO224 reinforced-isolated amplifier was used, implemented according to the recommended application circuit in the datasheet~\cite{noauthor_iso224_nodate}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}b. Both front-ends operated from a \SI{5}{\volt} supply, ensuring that all signals remained well within the ISO224's allowable input range of \SI{\pm 12}{\volt}. The ISO224 had a fixed gain of \(1/3\), determined by component availability rather than by a specific design target. On the non-isolated side, the output was differential with a common-mode voltage of \( V_{DD2}/2 = \SI{2.5}{\volt} \). A unity-gain differential amplifier (MAX44241AUK+T, using four matched \SI{10}{\kilo\ohm} resistors) converted this to a single-ended signal, effectively canceling the \SI{2.5}{\volt} offset, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:isolation_blocks}c.

The \gls{daq} input range was configured to \SI{\pm 5}{\volt}, chosen to provide sufficient headroom for the anticipated signals while optimizing ADC resolution. The validation of the isolated system is addressed in Section~\ref{sec:validation}.


\subsection{Power Supply}
\label{sec:PSU}
The circuit operated from a USB-C power supply, which provided \SI{5}{\volt} on the non-isolated ground (GND) domain. Each front-end derived its own isolated rail from this supply using the NXE2S0505MC-R7 DC-DC converters, as described in Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}. The \gls{daq} card was powered independently from the mains, with no shared power path with the circuit. The power supply building blocks were illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}.

\subsubsection{USB-C Power Supply}
The \SI{5}{\volt} rail was derived from the VBUS pin of a USB-C receptacle, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}a. Two \SI{5.1}{\kilo\ohm} pull-down resistors on CC1 and CC2 identified the circuit as a power sink to the host, prompting it to supply \SI{5}{\volt} without requiring active power delivery negotiation~\cite{noauthor_usb_nodate}. The data lines and sideband use (SBU) pins were left unconnected, as the connector was used solely for power.

\subsubsection{EMG Power Supply}
\label{sec:EMG_power}
The \gls{emg} front-end required a dual-rail supply for the INA828 and signal-conditioning operational amplifiers (op-amps). A TPS65133DPDR generated \SI{\pm 5}{\volt} from the isolated \SI{5}{\volt} input on GND\_G, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}b. The output filter adhered to the recommended application circuit from the datasheet~\cite{noauthor_tps65133_nodate}, with $L_{G1} = L_{G2} = \SI{4.7}{\micro\henry}$ and decoupling capacitors on both rails.

\subsubsection{EDA Power Supply}
\label{sec:EDA_power}
The \gls{eda} front-end operated on a single \SI{3.3}{\volt} rail. An AMS1117-3.3 regulator converted the isolated \SI{5}{\volt} from GND\_A to \SI{3.3}{\volt}, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:power_blocks}c. An input capacitor $C_{A9} = \SI{10}{\micro\farad}$ and an output capacitor $C_{A10} = \SI{22}{\micro\farad}$ followed the recommended application circuit from the datasheet~\cite{evvo_ams1117_nodate}. This rail served as VDD for both the current source and the virtual ground divider in the \gls{eda} front-end.

\section{Validation Protocol}
\label{sec:validation}

This section describes the system designed to measure \gls{emg} simultaneously and \gls{eda} at the same muscle site. Validation proceeds in two stages: individual circuit verification in Sessions 1 and 2, followed by simultaneous measurement validation in Sessions 3 to 5.

\subsection{Validation Questions}
\label{sec:validation_Q}
The validation was organized around seven questions that structure the analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:signal_processing}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the signal characteristics of the \gls{emg} front-end during isolated muscle contraction, and what are its limitations?
    \item What are the signal characteristics of the \gls{eda} front-end during isolated measurement, and what are its limitations?
    \item How does the galvanic isolation stage affect signal amplitude, frequency content, waveform morphology, and added noise?
    \item How does digital post-filtering affect \gls{emg} signal quality at the pre- and post-isolation stages?
    \item How does simultaneous \gls{emg} acquisition affect the \gls{eda} measurement?
    \item How does simultaneous \gls{eda} acquisition affect the \gls{emg} measurement?
    \item How consistent are the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} measurements across repeated simultaneous recording sessions?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Participants}
The participant was one healthy adult (the author), right-handed, with no known neuromuscular or dermatological disorders affecting the hands. The recordings constituted a technical self-experiment conducted solely for circuit validation. Since the validation involved no clinical intervention and no participant outside the author, no formal ethics approval was sought.

A custom Python script, \texttt{daq\_gui\_multi\_validation.py}, handled data acquisition throughout all sessions. \subsection{Electrode Placement and Skin Preparation}

\subsubsection{Skin Preparation}

Skin preparation was kept minimal to preserve the skin's electrical properties at the EDA sites. The same minimal protocol was applied at the EMG sites, despite the standard recommendation of alcohol cleaning and light abrasion for EMG recordings~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010, konrad_abc_2006}. Because the EMG and EDA electrodes shared the palm, a unified protocol was required, and protecting the EDA signal took priority. The participant washed both hands with soap and water and dried them with a paper towel. No lotion or skin product was applied on the morning of the recording. Jewelry was removed from the left hand and wrist.

\subsubsection{Electrode Placement}
\label{sec:electrode_placement}
This thesis targeted the biceps as the clinical deployment site, with EMG and EDA electrodes co-located on the same muscle. For technical validation, the left palm was used instead. The hypothenar eminence (the muscular base of the little finger) and the thenar eminence (the muscular base of the thumb) are established electrode sites for palmar EDA~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012, tronstad_current_2022}. The thenar eminence also carries voluntary thumb muscles that produce reliable sEMG bursts on demand. The palm, therefore, provided both a known EDA reference signal and a cooperative EMG source. This allowed front-end performance to be benchmarked before future deployment at the biceps.

Three electrodes were used for EMG and two for EDA, with positions shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hand_electrode_placement}. Two different electrode types were used, one per modality: Ambu BlueSensor Q wet-gel electrodes for EMG, and Kendall H135SG solid-gel electrodes for EDA. The wet gel of the Ambu electrode provides low electrode-skin impedance favorable for EMG, while the solid gel of the Kendall electrode avoids gel migration into the stratum corneum that would otherwise bias the EDA conductance reading~\cite{tronstad_current_2022, martinsen_sources_2015}. 

For EMG, two recording electrodes were placed over the thenar eminence in a bipolar configuration. They were aligned along the length of the thumb muscles with an edge-to-edge inter-electrode distance of \qty{10}{\milli\meter}\footnote{The recommended inter-electrode distance of \qty{20}{\milli\meter} for large muscle bellies~\cite{konrad_abc_2006} was reduced to \qty{10}{\milli\meter} to keep both electrodes within the thenar muscle belly, which is considerably smaller than the muscles on which the recommendation is based.}. The EMG ground electrode was placed on the back of the left wrist, at the position where a wristwatch would normally sit.

For EDA, two electrodes were placed on the hypothenar eminence of the left palm with a center-to-center distance of \qty{10}{\milli\meter}\footnote{Inter-electrode distances in this thesis follow the convention used in each literature tradition: edge-to-edge for EMG and center-to-center for EDA.}. This left the thenar site available for the EMG pair.

\subsection{Stimulation Instructions}

%Stimulation cue onset timestamps were saved as event markers in the TDMS file during acquisition.
\paragraph{EMG Stimulation}
The participant sat with the left forearm resting on the table, elbow flexed approximately \SI{90}{\degree} and the palm facing up. The fingers were relaxed in a neutral, slightly open position. 

An FSR400 force sensor was placed on top of the index finger before each block and remained in position throughout. At each EMG stimulus cue, the participant brought the thumb tip into contact with the index finger tip as quickly as possible and held the contraction for \SI{1}{\second}. A multimeter connected to the FSR400 provided real-time resistance feedback, and the participant adjusted force to maintain the sensor resistance at approximately \SI{5}{\kilo\ohm} during each contraction. Based on the FSR400 datasheet~\cite{inc_fsr_nodate}, \SI{5}{\kilo\ohm} corresponds to approximately \SI{1}{\newton} of applied force (100~gf). After \SI{1}{\second}, the participant relaxed the hand and returned to the neutral position.

\paragraph{EDA Stimulation}
At each \gls{eda} stimulus cue, the participant inhaled deeply over 2~seconds from normal breathing, then exhaled normally and relaxed. Simultaneous visual and auditory cues from the control software guided the timing of the deep breath.

\subsection{Recording Procedure}

All sessions followed the same structure: a 30-minute stabilization period during which the final 10 minutes were recorded as baseline, followed by a 30-minute test run. Sessions 1 and 2 verified each circuit individually, with only one circuit connected at a time. Sessions 3 to 5 validated simultaneous operation with both circuits connected.

\subsubsection{Session 1: EMG Circuit Verification} \label{sec:session1}
Only the EMG circuit was connected. The EDA circuit was physically disconnected. Two channels were recorded simultaneously throughout:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pre-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
    \item Post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Electrodes were applied as described above (EMG electrodes only).
    \item The participant rested quietly for 30~minutes to allow the electrode-skin impedance to stabilize.
    \item During the final 10~minutes of this period, baseline \gls{emg} was recorded on both channels.
    \item The 30-minute test run began. Every 15~seconds, a cue prompted the participant to perform one \gls{emg} stimulus of \SI{1}{\second}, followed by relaxation. This yielded 120 \gls{emg} stimuli.
\end{enumerate}

Post-session analysis of Session~1 data is described in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:comparative_analysis}.

\subsubsection{Session 2: EDA Circuit Verification} \label{sec:session2}
Only the \gls{eda} circuit was connected. The \gls{emg} circuit was physically disconnected. Two channels were recorded simultaneously throughout:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pre-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
    \item Post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Electrodes were applied as described above (EDA electrodes only).
    \item The participant rested quietly for 30~minutes to allow the electrode-skin impedance to stabilize.
    \item During the final 10~minutes of this period, baseline EDA was recorded on both channels. The post-isolation baseline served as the session-level EDA noise floor in the analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.
    \item The 30-minute test run began. Every 60~seconds, a cue prompted the participant to perform one deep-breath EDA stimulus. This yielded 30 EDA stimuli.
\end{enumerate}

Post-session analysis of Session~2 data is described in Sections~\ref{sec:eda_processing} and~\ref{sec:comparative_analysis}.

\subsubsection{Sessions 3 to 5: Simultaneous EMG and EDA} \label{sec:session3-5}
Both circuits were connected simultaneously. Unlike Sessions 1 and 2, which recorded pre- and post-isolation channels in parallel, only the post-isolation channels were recorded in Sessions 3 to 5:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \gls{emg} post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
    \item \gls{eda} post-isolation, unfiltered (raw)
\end{itemize}
Each session consisted of a 30-minute stabilization period during which the final 10 minutes were recorded as baseline, followed by a 30-minute test run.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} electrodes were applied as described above.
    \item The participant rested quietly for 30 minutes to allow the electrode-skin impedance to stabilize.
    \item During the final 10~minutes of this period, baseline \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} signals were recorded while the participant remained still and breathed normally. The post-isolation \gls{eda} baseline served as the session-level \gls{eda} noise floor in the analysis described in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}. The \gls{emg} baseline was recorded as a sanity check and was not used as a noise floor. \gls{emg} noise is estimated per epoch as described in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}.
    \item The 30-minute test run consisted of three 10-minute blocks:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Block 1: EDA-only (10 minutes)}\\ The participant remained seated with the left hand resting in the neutral position. Every 60 seconds, a cue prompted one deep-breath \gls{eda} stimulus. No voluntary hand or finger movement was performed. This block yielded 10 \gls{eda} stimuli.
        \item \textbf{Block 2: EMG-only (10 minutes)}\\ Every 15~seconds, a cue prompted one \gls{emg} stimulus of \SI{1}{\second}, followed by relaxation. No deep-breath \gls{eda} stimuli were performed. This block yielded 40 \gls{emg} stimuli.
        \item \textbf{Block 3: Combined EMG and EDA (10 minutes)}\\ Both stimulus types were performed according to the same timing as Blocks 1 and 2. Within each 60-second \gls{eda} cycle, the \gls{eda} cue fired first and four \gls{emg} cues followed at 15-second intervals, with the first \gls{emg} cue delayed by \SI{5}{\second} relative to the \gls{eda} cue. This block yielded 10 \gls{eda} stimuli and 40 \gls{emg} stimuli.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item In total, one session yielded 20 \gls{eda} stimuli (10 in EDA-only + 10 in combined) and 80 \gls{emg} stimuli (40 in EMG-only + 40 in combined).
    \item Sessions 3 to 5 were conducted on three separate days under the same conditions.
    \item The block order was fixed across Sessions 3 to 5: EDA-only first, EMG-only second, and Combined last.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Core Analysis Methods}
\label{sec:core_analysis}
% Section pipeline: overview → RMS → SNR → amplitude preservation → Pearson → PSD → handoff.
% Chapter role: defines the five signal-agnostic analysis methods reused in results and discussion.

% Section overview and forward pointer
%Five methods used: RMS, SNR, amplitude preservation, Pearson correlation, PSD - signal-agnostic definitions only
Five methods were used throughout this thesis to answer Q1 to Q7 (Section~\ref{sec:validation_Q}): \gls{rms}, \gls{snr}, amplitude preservation, Pearson correlation, and \gls{psd}. Each is defined here in signal-agnostic terms. Signal-specific implementations are given in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

% RMS: definition and EMG/EDA applicability
%RMS definition and equation - EMG only, state this explicitly, why not EDA
\gls{rms} quantified the average amplitude of a signal over a fixed time window. For a discrete signal $x[n]$ over $N$ samples, \gls{rms} is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \text{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x[n]^2}
    \label{eq:rms}
\end{equation}
\gls{rms} was applied only to the \gls{emg} signal. The \gls{emg} signal oscillates symmetrically around zero, so the arithmetic mean is uninformative. Squaring each sample before averaging removes the sign and yields a direct amplitude measure. \gls{rms} was not applied to \gls{eda}. The DC-coupled \gls{eda} signal has a large non-zero mean from the tonic skin conductance level, so an \gls{rms} computed over \gls{eda} reflects that mean rather than the noise floor and carries no useful noise information. \gls{rms} is the standard amplitude measure in surface \gls{emg} literature~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}.

% SNR: definition and purpose
%SNR - general equation, S and N deferred
\gls{snr} quantified the amplitude of a desired signal relative to the background noise floor and indicated whether the stimulus-evoked signal was distinguishable from baseline noise:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{S}{N}\right)
    \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the signal amplitude and $N$ is the noise floor. The definitions of $S$ and $N$ differ between \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} and are given in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

% Amplitude preservation: isolation amplitude check
%Amplitude preservation - generic X, defined per signal downstream, used in Q1
Amplitude preservation quantified how well the isolation stage preserved signal amplitude across the ISO224. The ISO224 introduced a fixed gain of $1/3$ that was compensated in software before this metric was computed (Section~\ref{sec:galvanic_isolation}). A value of \SI{0}{\decibel} indicated perfect amplitude preservation:
\begin{equation}
    A_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{X_\text{post}}{X_\text{pre}}\right)
    \label{eq:amplitude_preservation}
\end{equation}
where $X$ is an amplitude measure defined per signal in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

% Pearson correlation: morphology preservation check
%Pearson correlation - morphology preservation, used in Q1
Pearson correlation quantified the linear similarity between two signals~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. For two discrete signals $x[n]$ and $y[n]$ of length $N$, the Pearson correlation coefficient\footnote{Kamen and Gabriel~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010} present the same expression as the cross-correlation function evaluated at zero lag, the form common in EMG signal-processing literature. The two are equivalent terms for the same quantity.} $r$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    r = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(x[n] - \bar{x}\right)\left(y[n] - \bar{y}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(x[n] - \bar{x}\right)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(y[n] - \bar{y}\right)^2}}
    \label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ are the sample means. The coefficient $r$ ranges from $-1$ to $+1$. A value of $r = +1$ indicates an identical waveform shape. Pearson $r$ was used in Q3 to verify that the isolation stage preserved signal morphology independently of the amplitude preservation check defined in Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation}.

% PSD: spectral analysis via Welch's method with per-segment detrending + section handoff
%PSD - Welch's method, per-segment detrending, pointers to signal-specific params
\gls{psd} described the distribution of signal power across frequency. The estimate used Welch's method~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}, which averaged periodograms of overlapping windowed segments to reduce spectral variance at the cost of frequency resolution. The segments overlapped by 50\%. Each segment was mean-subtracted before the Fourier transform (the default behavior of \texttt{scipy.signal.welch}), which removed the DC contribution of the voltage lifting circuit (Section~\ref{sec:EMG_frontend}) from the \gls{emg} spectrum. Window length was set per signal in Sections~\ref{sec:emg_processing} and~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

Three types of \gls{psd} were used: active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked. The active-window \gls{psd} captured the signal spectrum during stimulus response and was computed from the contraction window for \gls{emg} and the \gls{scr} detection window for \gls{eda}. The stabilization \gls{psd} captured the chain noise floor at rest and was computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} pre-session stabilization recording. The cue-masked \gls{psd} captured the chain noise floor during the test phase and was computed from the inter-cue windows of each recording. The following two sections describe how each method was applied to the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} signals, respectively.
%A session-wide RMS would collapse the entire recording to a single power estimate and lose temporal resolution. Moving the window sample by sample yielded one RMS value per sample, preserving the time course of muscle activity.

%EMG is bipolar, so the signal was rectified before the window was applied. Without rectification, positive and negative values would partially cancel within each window, underestimating the true signal amplitude.
\subsection{EMG Signal Processing}
\label{sec:emg_processing}
%Pipeline order: epoch extraction → mean subtraction → scalar RMS → Q4 branch → contamination filter → noise floor → sliding window RMS → S and N definitions → PSD parameters

The following section describes, in pipeline order, how the core analysis methods were applied to the EMG signal.

%Epoch extraction (-0.5 to +3.5s), contraction window (0-1.2s), pre-cue (-0.5 to 0s), recovery for visualisation
The continuous \gls{emg} recording was segmented into epochs time-locked to each contraction cue. Each epoch spanned from \SI{0.5}{\second} before to \SI{3.5}{\second} after the cue onset, giving a \SI{4}{\second} window per contraction. Cue onset timestamps were extracted from the event markers stored in the TDMS file during acquisition. Two sub-windows were defined within each epoch: the pre-cue window from \SIrange{-0.5}{0}{\second} relative to the cue for DC offset estimation and noise floor computation, and the contraction window from \SIrange{0.0}{1.0}{\second} for signal amplitude estimation. The remainder of the epoch from \SI{1.0}{\second} to \SI{3.5}{\second} was included in figures to display the post-contraction return to baseline

%Per-epoch mean subtraction - pre-cue mean, not full epoch, reason
After extraction, the DC offset was removed from each epoch individually. The mean of the pre-cue window was computed and subtracted from all samples in that epoch. Using the pre-cue window rather than the full epoch mean avoided bias from the contraction, which would otherwise elevate the epoch mean and suppress both the baseline and contraction estimates. This step removed static DC contributions from the voltage lifting circuit (Section~\ref{sec:EMG_frontend}), electrode half-cell potentials, and amplifier input offsets.

%Scalar RMS - two values per epoch
Two scalar RMS values were computed per epoch using Equation~\ref{eq:rms}. The pre-cue RMS was calculated across all samples within the pre-cue window and served as the per-epoch noise-floor estimate. The contraction RMS was calculated over all samples in the contraction window and served as the signal amplitude estimate.

%Q4 parallel path - filter chain after mean subtraction, zero-phase, notch BW
To address Q4, a three-stage digital filter chain was applied offline to the continuous signal before epoch extraction, creating a parallel path alongside the unfiltered signal. Per-epoch mean subtraction was applied to both paths after extraction. The chain consisted of a second-order Butterworth HPF at \SI{20}{\hertz}, an IIR notch at \SI{50}{\hertz} with $Q = 30$, and a fourth-order Butterworth LPF at \SI{500}{\hertz}. All stages used zero-phase filtering via \texttt{filtfilt}, which applied each filter in the forward and backward directions to eliminate phase distortion. Zero-phase filtering was not available in the real-time analog chain because it requires access to future samples. The digital notch bandwidth was
\begin{equation}
    \Delta f = \frac{f_0}{Q} = \frac{\SI{50}{\hertz}}{30} \approx \SI{1.67}{\hertz}
    \label{eq:digital_notch_bw}
\end{equation}
%This was narrower than the analog twin-T notch bandwidth of \SI{10.5}{\hertz} ($Q \approx 5$, Equation~\ref{eq:notch_q}), preserving more EMG spectral content around \SI{50}{\hertz}. 
This was narrower than the analog twin-T notch bandwidth of \SI{10.5}{\hertz} ($Q \approx 5$, Equation~\ref{eq:notch_q}). All subsequent processing steps were applied identically to both paths.

%Contamination filter - empirical threshold, no citation
A contamination filter was applied to exclude epochs with elevated baseline activity before metric computation. An epoch was rejected if its pre-cue RMS exceeded twice the median pre-cue RMS across epochs in the same acquisition block ($\text{RMS}_\text{pre} > 2 \times \tilde{\text{RMS}}_\text{pre}$). This threshold was chosen empirically. No standard criterion exists in the EMG literature for this type of rejection. Epochs above the threshold were assumed to contain baseline interference that would corrupt both the noise-floor and contraction-RMS estimates.
%Epochs above the threshold likely contained motion artifacts or other baseline interference that would corrupt both the noise floor and contraction RMS estimates.

%Noise floor - mean pre-cue RMS across clean epochs, why per-epoch
The noise floor was the mean pre-cue RMS across all epochs that passed the contamination filter. This per-epoch approach tracked signal conditions at each contraction rather than relying on a session-level baseline that would not capture short-term changes in electrode contact or ambient noise.

%Sliding window RMS - linear envelope, visualization only, clean epochs only
Linear envelope detection was used to visualize the time course of muscle activation~\cite{kamen_essentials_2010}. The rectified EMG signal was processed with a \SI{100}{\milli\second} sliding RMS window, producing a smooth amplitude envelope that tracked muscle activity over time~\cite{konrad_abc_2006}. The \SI{100}{\milli\second} window balanced temporal resolution against stability of estimates. The sliding-window RMS was applied only to clean epochs and was not used in any metric computation.

%S and N definitions for EMG SNR - computation moved to comparative analysis
For EMG, $S$ in Equation~\ref{eq:snr} was the mean contraction RMS across clean epochs, and $N$ was the noise floor as defined above.

%PSD parameters for EMG
EMG used the three \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:core_analysis}. The active-window \gls{psd} was computed per epoch over the \SI{1}{\second} contraction window with a segment length of \SI{1}{\second}, and the per-epoch spectra were averaged across clean epochs. The stabilization \gls{psd} was computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} pre-session stabilization recording with a segment length of \SI{1}{\second}. The cue-masked \gls{psd} was computed from the inter-cue windows of the EMG recording with a segment length of \SI{1}{\second}. Each cue was excluded from $-\SI{0.5}{\second}$ to $+\SI{4.0}{\second}$ relative to cue onset.

%Mains harmonic power - quantify residual power-line interference
Mains harmonic power was computed from the active-window \gls{psd} to quantify residual power-line interference in the \gls{emg} signal. Three harmonics were evaluated: the \SI{50}{\hertz} fundamental, and the \SI{100}{\hertz} and \SI{150}{\hertz} harmonics. For each harmonic, the power was integrated across a band of $\pm\Delta f$ around the center frequency, with $\Delta f$ from Equation~\ref{eq:digital_notch_bw}:
\begin{equation}
    P_\text{harmonic}(f_0) = \int_{f_0 - \Delta f}^{f_0 + \Delta f} \text{PSD}(f)\, df
     \label{eq:harmonic_power}
\end{equation}
where $f_0$ is the harmonic center frequency and $\Delta f = \SI{1.67}{\hertz}$. The total mains harmonic power was the sum across the three harmonics. A low total harmonic power would indicate that the EMG front-end effectively rejected power-line interference. Elevated power at \SI{100}{\hertz} or \SI{150}{\hertz} would indicate that harmonic content described in Section~\ref{sec:emg_noise} was not suppressed by the \SI{50}{\hertz} notch filter. The EDA signal required a different processing approach due to its DC-coupled nature and slower response dynamics, as described in the following section.


\subsection{EDA Signal Processing}
\label{sec:eda_processing}
%Pipeline order: epoch extraction → no filtering → no mean subtraction → SCL → noise floor → SCR detection → epoch averaging → SNR definition

The following section describes how the EDA signal was quantified. As described in Section~\ref{sec:EDA_signal}, the EDA signal consists of a tonic component, the skin conductance level (SCL), and a phasic component, the skin conductance response (SCR). The sampled voltage $V_\text{EDA}$ was converted to skin conductance using Equation~\ref{eq:EDA_skin_resistance}, with measurements expressed in \si{\micro\siemens}.

%Epoch extraction (-2 to +10s), baseline -2 to 0s, why 12s window
The EDA recording was segmented into epochs time-locked to each breath-holding cue, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}A. Each epoch spanned from \SI{2}{\second} before to \SI{10}{\second} after the cue onset, giving a \SI{12}{\second} window per trial. The pre-cue segment from \SIrange{-2}{0}{\second} served as the per-epoch baseline for SCL and noise floor estimation. A longer pre-cue window than in the EMG case was used because EDA exhibits slower drift and requires more samples to obtain a stable tonic estimate. The post-cue window extended to \SI{10}{\second} to capture the full SCR morphology, including the recovery phase, which typically lasts several seconds after the response peak.

%No digital filtering + reason
No digital filtering was applied to the EDA signal. The \SI{1.5}{\hertz} analog low-pass filter in the EDA front-end (Section~\ref{sec:EDA_frontend}) already bandlimits the signal to the relevant EDA frequency range. Additional digital filtering risked distorting the slow SCR waveform dynamics that carried the physiological information of interest.

%No mean subtraction + reason
Mean subtraction was not applied to the EDA signal. Unlike EMG, EDA is DC-coupled, and its mean reflects the tonic skin conductance level. Removing the epoch mean would discard the SCL, which was itself a metric of interest.

%SCL - per-epoch pre-cue mean, why per-epoch, use in Q5
The SCL was computed as the mean conductance over the per-epoch pre-cue window, shown as the grey region in Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}B. This operationalization differs from the conventional session-level SCL estimate~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012} and was chosen to provide a tonic estimate representative of signal conditions at the time of each stimulus, while capturing habituation-driven drift across the recording. The SCL also served as a between-condition comparator in Q5 to detect tonic contamination from simultaneous EMG operation.

%Noise floor - two estimates, session-level circuit + per-epoch local, why not RMS
Two noise floor estimates were used for the EDA signal. The first was a session-level estimate computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} stabilization recording at the start of each session, during which the subject sat still with no stimulus. The recording was linearly detrended to remove residual SCL drift, and the standard deviation of the detrended residual was taken as the session-level noise floor $\sigma_\text{baseline, session}$. A separate $\sigma_\text{baseline, session}$ was computed for the pre- and post-isolation channels, since these correspond to different physical circuits with potentially different noise characteristics. This estimate represented the circuit noise floor of each EDA front-end stage under quiet conditions.

The second was a per-epoch estimate computed from the \SI{2}{\second} pre-cue window of each stimulus epoch. Each window was linearly detrended, and the standard deviation of the residual was computed. The mean across all epochs in a block was taken as the local noise floor $\sigma_\text{baseline, local}$. This estimate represented the noise conditions immediately preceding each SCR, including circuit noise and non-specific activity. For brevity, $\sigma_\text{baseline,session}$ and $\sigma_\text{baseline,local}$ are abbreviated as $\sigma_\text{session}$ and $\sigma_\text{local}$ in the remainder of the thesis.

RMS was not used for either estimate. EDA is DC-coupled, and an RMS over the baseline is dominated by the SCL mean and carries no noise-floor information.

%SCR detection - Zangróniz criteria, onset, 250ms smoothing, unsmoothed amplitude, dominant response
SCR detection followed the criteria described by~\textcite{zangroniz_electrodermal_2017}. A response was considered valid if its amplitude exceeded \SI{0.05}{\micro\siemens} and its onset fell within the \SIrange{1}{6}{\second} post-cue detection window shown as the green region in Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}B. The \gls{scr} onset was identified as the conductance minimum within \SIrange{1}{3.5}{\second} post-cue, or up to the peak location if the peak fell before \SI{3.5}{\second}. The onset search used the smoothed signal to match the peak-detection procedure described below. Restricting the onset to this sub-window enforced physiologically plausible stimulus-response latency. The peak was still permitted anywhere within the full \SIrange{1}{6}{\second} detection window. To reduce bias from noise on the peak location, the signal was smoothed with a \SI{250}{\milli\second} moving average before applying the argmax. The window suppressed noise spikes that would otherwise shift the peak location away from the physiological SCR peak without smearing the SCR itself. The SCR rise time is typically several seconds. The window length was chosen empirically. The SCR amplitude was read from the smoothed signal at the detected peak index (Figure~\ref{fig:eda_epoch}B). For consistency with the peak-detection procedure, the amplitude was read from the smoothed signal rather than the unsmoothed one. The same smoothing was applied to the pre-isolation signal to keep the comparison symmetrical. The amplitude effect of smoothing on the pre-isolation signal is negligible because it carries no broadband noise. The SCR amplitude was computed as the peak-to-onset conductance difference. Where multiple threshold crossings occurred within a single epoch, only the dominant response was kept.

%Epoch averaging - noise suppression argument for post-isolation signal
The SCR amplitude reported for each block was the mean across all detected epochs within that block. Averaging across stimulus-locked epochs reduced the contribution of stimulus-uncorrelated noise sources, since such noise cancels in the mean while the physiological response does not.

%Averaging across stimulus-locked epochs reduced the noise contribution from the isolation stage, since broadband noise is uncorrelated with the stimulus and cancels in the mean, while the physiological response does not.

%EDA SNR - two definitions, circuit and local, diagnostic meaning
Two EDA SNR values were computed per condition using the noise floors defined above. The circuit SNR used the session-level noise floor as the denominator:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_\text{EDA,circuit} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{A_\text{SCR}}{\sigma_\text{session}}\right)
    \label{eq:eda_snr_circuit}
\end{equation}
The local SNR used the per-epoch noise floor:
\begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_\text{EDA,local} = 20\log_{10}\left(\frac{A_\text{SCR}}{\sigma_\text{local}}\right)
    \label{eq:eda_snr_local}
\end{equation}
In both equations, $A_\text{SCR}$ was the mean SCR amplitude across detected epochs within the condition. A gap between the two SNR values within the same condition would indicate that most of the per-epoch noise came from subject activity rather than from the circuit.

EDA used the three \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:core_analysis}. The active-window \gls{psd} was computed per epoch over the \SIrange{1}{6}{\second} \gls{scr} detection window with a segment length of \SI{5}{\second}, and the per-epoch spectra were averaged across all epochs. The stabilization \gls{psd} was computed from the \SI{10}{\minute} pre-session stabilization recording with a segment length of \SI{10}{\second}. The cue-masked \gls{psd} was computed from the inter-cue windows of the EDA recording with a segment length of \SI{10}{\second}. Each cue was excluded from $-\SI{2.0}{\second}$ to $+\SI{10.0}{\second}$ relative to cue onset. The \SI{10}{\second} segment gave a frequency resolution of \SI{0.1}{\hertz}.

Section~\ref{sec:comparative_analysis} describes how these metrics were combined across sessions and conditions to answer Q1 to Q7.


\subsection{Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:comparative_analysis}
The validation protocol addressed seven questions, Q1 to Q7, each corresponding to a distinct aspect of circuit performance. Questions Q1 to Q4 characterized the individual front-ends and the isolation stage separately, while Q5 to Q7 evaluated system behavior during simultaneous operation. Tables~\ref{tab:validation_overview_a} and~\ref{tab:validation_overview_b} summarize the sessions, conditions, and metrics for each question. The seven questions together provide a comprehensive characterization of the front-end, from individual signal quality to cross-circuit interference to session-to-session consistency.

Pooling across Sessions 3 to 5 followed two patterns. For Q5, Q5b, Q6, and Q6b, per-epoch arrays were combined across the three sessions, and the pooled mean and sample standard deviation were computed across the combined arrays. \subsubsection{Q1 - EMG Front-End Characterization}
\label{sec:q1_emg_frontend_char}

Q1 evaluated the signal quality of the EMG front-end during muscle contractions. The post-isolation signal from Session~1 was used, recorded with the EDA circuit physically disconnected. Session~1 contained 120 contraction epochs, each spanning \SIrange{-0.5}{+3.5}{\second} relative to the stimulation cue.

The noise floor was estimated as the mean pre-cue RMS across clean epochs, and the contraction RMS was computed over the 0 to \SI{1.0}{\second} window, following Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}. SNR was computed as the ratio of contraction RMS to pre-cue RMS in decibels and averaged across all clean epochs using Equation~\ref{eq:snr}, to quantify how well the front-end resolves voluntary muscle activity against the ambient noise floor.

The active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing} were computed for Session~1 to characterize the \gls{emg} spectral content.

\subsubsection{Q2 - EDA Front-End Characterization}
\label{sec:q2_eda_frontend_char}

Q2 evaluated the signal quality of the EDA front-end during electrodermal measurement. The post-isolation signal from Session~2 was used, recorded with the EMG circuit physically disconnected. Session~2 contained 30 stimulus epochs, each spanning \SIrange{-2}{+10}{\second} relative to the breath-holding cue.

The SCL, circuit noise floor, and local noise floor were computed following Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}. SCR amplitude, rise time, and detection rate were extracted from each epoch and averaged across all detected epochs. The circuit SNR and local SNR were computed using Equations~\ref{eq:eda_snr_circuit} and~\ref{eq:eda_snr_local}, to quantify the front-end's ability to resolve phasic SCR responses above the hardware noise floor and to exceed the SCR threshold consistently across trials. The active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing} were computed for Session~2 to characterize the \gls{eda} spectral content.

\subsubsection{Q3 - Isolation Stage Effect (EMG and EDA)}
\label{sec:q3_isolation_stage}

Q3 evaluated the effect of the galvanic isolation stage on the EMG and EDA signals across amplitude, frequency content, and waveform morphology. Data from Session~1 were used for the EMG comparison and data from Session~2 for the EDA comparison. Pearson $r$ was computed per epoch between the gain-corrected pre- and post-isolation signals using Equation~\ref{eq:pearson}, to assess whether the isolation stage preserved waveform morphology. Pearson $r$ was computed over the full epoch window: EMG \SIrange{-0.5}{3.5}{\second} and EDA \SIrange{-2}{10}{\second}. The mean and standard deviation were reported across clean epochs for EMG. For EDA, the mean, standard deviation, and median were reported across all epochs.

For EMG, amplitude preservation was computed using Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation}, with $X$ defined as the mean contraction RMS across clean epochs, to assess amplitude preservation across the isolation barrier.

For \gls{emg}, amplitude preservation across the isolation barrier was computed using Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation} with $X$ defined as the mean contraction RMS across clean epochs. SNR was computed pre- and post-isolation using the mean pre-cue RMS as the noise floor to assess whether the isolator degraded the EMG signal. The active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing} were computed for the pre- and post-isolation \gls{emg} channels of Session~1 to compare the two channels.

For EDA, amplitude preservation was computed using Equation~\ref{eq:amplitude_preservation} with mean SCR amplitude as $X$. The circuit SNR, local SNR, and SCR detection rate were compared pre- and post-isolation using Equations~\ref{eq:eda_snr_circuit} and~\ref{eq:eda_snr_local}, to assess whether the ISO224 suppressed EDA phasic activity beyond the compensated gain. The active-window, stabilization, and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing} were computed for the pre- and post-isolation \gls{eda} channels of Session~2 to compare the two channels.

\subsubsection{Q4 - Digital Filtering Effect (EMG Only)}
\label{sec:q4_digital_filtering}

Q4 evaluated how offline digital filtering affected EMG signal quality at both the pre- and post-isolation stages. Four signals were derived from the two raw channels recorded in Session~1: pre-isolation raw, pre-isolation filtered, post-isolation raw, and post-isolation filtered. All digital filtering was applied offline using the filter chain described in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}.

The noise floor and SNR were computed for each of the four signals using the mean pre-cue RMS across clean epochs as the noise floor and contraction RMS as the signal amplitude, following Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}. The notch depth at \SI{50}{\hertz} was computed for each filter chain (pre- and post-isolation) using
\begin{equation}
    D_\text{notch}(f_0) = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_\text{raw}(f_0)}{P_\text{filtered}(f_0)}\right)
    \label{eq:notch_depth}
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the mains harmonic power at the harmonic centered on $f_0$ from Equation~\ref{eq:harmonic_power}, evaluated at $f_0 = \SI{50}{\hertz}$. This yielded two notch-depth values, one for the pre-isolation chain and one for the post-isolation chain. The mains harmonic power was computed at \SI{50}{\hertz}, \SI{100}{\hertz}, and \SI{150}{\hertz} using Equation~\ref{eq:harmonic_power} to quantify the per-harmonic suppression achieved by each filter chain. SNR, notch depth, and harmonic power comparisons between filtered and raw signals were used to quantify the signal quality recoverable through digital filtering.

The active-window and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing} were computed and compared across the four conditions.

A single clean epoch was also shown, without envelope detection, to visualize the filter's effect on the raw EMG waveform.

\subsubsection{Q5 - EMG Effect on EDA}
\label{sec:q5_emg_effect_on_eda}

Q5 evaluated whether simultaneous \gls{emg} operation contaminated the \gls{eda} signal. Three conditions were compared: Session~2 EDA-only as the clean reference, the EDA-only block from Sessions 3 to 5, and the Combined block from Sessions 3 to 5.

The \gls{scl} was computed as the per-epoch pre-cue mean and compared across all three conditions to detect any tonic offset introduced by simultaneous \gls{emg} operation, following Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}. The \gls{scl} shift was computed as the per-session difference between Combined and EDA-only mean \gls{scl}, averaged across Sessions 3 to 5 with sample standard deviation across $n = 3$ sessions. \gls{scr} amplitude, detection rate, circuit \gls{snr}, and local \gls{snr} were computed for each condition using Equations~\ref{eq:eda_snr_circuit} and~\ref{eq:eda_snr_local}, to test whether the galvanic isolation stage prevents \gls{emg} interference from reaching the \gls{scr}.

The active-window and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing} were computed for the Session~2 reference, the EDA-only block, and the Combined block to compare the three conditions. Active-window \gls{psd}s were pooled across Sessions 3 to 5 per condition, and cue-masked \gls{psd}s were averaged across Sessions 3 to 5 per condition.

\paragraph{Q5b - EDA Response at EMG Cues}
\label{sec:q5b_eda_at_emg_cues}
An extension of Q5 evaluated whether \gls{emg} contractions alone produced time-locked excursions in the \gls{eda} signal. The \gls{eda} signal from the EMG-only blocks of Sessions 3 to 5 was segmented into 120 time-locked epochs to \gls{emg} contraction cues, with 40 epochs per session. Each epoch spanned from \SI{2}{\second} before to \SI{3.5}{\second} after the cue onset. The deviation amplitude was defined as the signed peak-to-baseline excursion of the smoothed \gls{eda} signal over the \SIrange{0}{3}{\second} post-cue search window, with positive values indicating a downward deflection from baseline. The deviation latency was the time from cue onset to the deviation peak. The baseline was the mean of the smoothed signal over the \SIrange{-2}{0}{\second} pre-cue window, where smoothing followed the \SI{250}{\milli\second} moving-average procedure defined in Section~\ref{sec:eda_processing}.

\subsubsection{Q6 - EDA Effect on EMG}
\label{sec:q6_eda_effect_on_emg}

Q6 evaluated whether simultaneous \gls{eda} operation contaminated the \gls{emg} signal. Three conditions were compared: Session~1 EMG-only as the clean reference, the EMG-only block from Sessions 3 to 5, and the Combined block from Sessions 3 to 5.

The noise floor, contraction RMS, and SNR were computed for each condition using the mean pre-cue RMS across clean epochs as the noise floor, following Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}, to detect any interference introduced by the EDA excitation current into the EMG signal.

The active-window and cue-masked \gls{psd} types defined in Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing} were computed for the Session~1 reference, the EMG-only block, and the Combined block to compare the three conditions. Active-window \gls{psd}s were pooled across Sessions 3 to 5 per condition, and cue-masked \gls{psd}s were averaged across Sessions 3 to 5 per condition. \paragraph{Q6b - EMG Response at EDA Cues}
\label{sec:q6b_emg_at_eda_cues}
An extension of Q6 evaluated whether \gls{eda} stimuli alone produced time-locked excursions in the \gls{emg} signal. The \gls{emg} signal from the EDA-only block of Sessions 3 to 5 was segmented into 30 epochs time-locked to \gls{eda} stimulus cues, with 10 epochs per session. Each epoch spanned from \SI{2}{\second} before to \SI{5}{\second} after the cue onset, and the \gls{emg} envelope was constructed following Section~\ref{sec:emg_processing}. The deviation amplitude was defined as the signed maximum of the envelope minus baseline over the \SIrange{0}{5}{\second} post-cue search window, with positive values indicating an upward rise above baseline. This sign convention is the inverse of the Q5b convention defined in Section~\ref{sec:q5b_eda_at_emg_cues}. The deviation latency was the time from cue onset to the deviation peak, and the baseline was the mean of the envelope over the \SIrange{-2}{0}{\second} pre-cue window. RMS pre-cue and post-cue values were computed on the \gls{emg} envelope over matched \SI{2}{\second} windows spanning \SIrange{-2}{0}{\second} and \SIrange{0}{2}{\second}.

\subsubsection{Q7 - Reproducibility}
\label{sec:q7_reproducibility}

Q7 evaluated the consistency of simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} measurements across repeated sessions. Data from Sessions 3 to 5 were used, with all metrics computed per session and reported as mean~$\pm$~standard deviation across the three sessions.

For \gls{emg}, the noise floor, contraction RMS, and SNR were reported separately for the EMG-only and Combined blocks. Between-session standard deviations were used to assess front-end stability during repeated simultaneous operation in the presence of known sources of variability, such as electrode placement and skin preparation.

For EDA, the SCL, SCR amplitude, detection rate, circuit SNR, and local SNR were reported separately for the EDA-only and Combined blocks. SCL is known to vary with repeated stimulus exposure due to habituation~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012}. Between-session SCL variability is therefore expected and is not interpreted as circuit instability. Phasic metrics (SCR amplitude, detection rate, SNR) carry the front-end stability signal.
