\section{Background and Motivation}

% 1. The broad context: why multimodal physiological monitoring matters
No single physiological signal is sufficient to fully characterize a person's physiological state. A single signal can arise from multiple independent causes, making the underlying source difficult to identify from a single measurement. Physiological processes also operate on separate timescales. Muscle activation occurs within milliseconds of a motor command, while the autonomic nervous system responds over seconds to minutes. These limitations motivate the use of multiple signals acquired together, which is the principle behind multimodal physiological monitoring.

% 2. Brief introduction to EMG and EDA
\Gls{emg} records the electrical activity of contracting skeletal muscle through the peripheral nervous system. \Gls{eda} measures changes in skin electrical conductance driven by sweat gland activity, which is under direct control of the \gls{sns}~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012}. \gls{eda} can be measured in several ways, and the choice of measurement technique constrains both the electronics and the feasible co-location strategies. This thesis uses \gls{dc} exosomatic recording of skin conductance, with the choice discussed in Section~\ref{sec:EDA_measurement}. The two signals, therefore, capture independent physiological channels: \gls{emg} reflects somatic motor activity, while \gls{eda} reflects sympathetic autonomic activity. Because the two channels are independent, their combination carries information that neither signal alone provides. Multimodal approaches that integrate cardiac and electrodermal signals have improved classification accuracy compared to single-channel methods~\cite{affanni_wireless_2020, mcnaboe_design_2022}.

% 3. The clinical trigger: the Kildal study and the Bull recommendation
The clinical motivation for this thesis comes from the monitoring of non-verbal patients with spastic \gls{cp}, who cannot self-report pain or distress. \textcite{kildal_increased_2021} monitored \gls{hr} continuously in 14 non-communicating adults with intellectual disability during daily activities. Elevated \gls{hr} indicated pain or distress in 11 of the 14 participants. Several of these episodes occurred in situations not previously suspected to be stressful, including passive stretching of spastic limbs. \gls{hr} alone required contextual interpretation to be meaningful and is insufficient for detecting prolonged or chronic pain~\cite{kildal_increased_2021}. Building on this work, \textcite{bull_heart_2025} recommended skin conductance and muscle tension as candidate multimodal biomarkers for future studies in this population.

% 4. The co-location idea: biceps as the clinically motivated target
These recommendations point to \gls{eda} and \gls{emg} as the candidate modalities, and to the biceps as the clinically motivated measurement site. Passive stretching of spastic upper-limb muscles is a documented source of distress in this patient group~\cite{kildal_increased_2021}, and recording both signals on the biceps would capture the somatic and autonomic responses to the same physical event. The palm and fingers are the preferred \gls{eda} measurement sites because eccrine sweat gland density is highest there~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012}. Still, palmar placement is impractical in this patient group, as electrodes on the hand and fingers interfere with daily care and are prone to motion artifacts. Co-locating \gls{eda} with \gls{emg} on the biceps removes the palmar constraint. It provides temporal alignment between the somatic and autonomic responses to the same stimulus, supporting the interpretation of each signal in context.

% 5. The engineering problem: why co-location is not trivial
Co-locating the two modalities at the same site introduces electrical coupling between the two measurement circuits. The \gls{dc} excitation current injected by the \gls{eda} circuit can, in principle, introduce a \gls{dc} offset at the high-gain \gls{emg} input when the two circuits share a common ground. A second coupling path arises at the \gls{emg} reference electrode itself: if it offers a lower-impedance return path than the intended \gls{eda} reference, part of the excitation current can divert through the \gls{emg} chain and corrupt the measured skin conductance. Both paths require a shared conductive ground to close, so galvanic isolation between each front-end and the shared data acquisition stage separates the two circuits' ground domains and breaks both paths simultaneously.

% 6. The reshape: from biceps motivation to palm validation
The thesis is a collaboration between the Department of Clinical and Biomedical Engineering, the Department of Neurohabilitation at Oslo University Hospital, and the Department of Physics at the University of Oslo. The original proposal placed both signal chains on the biceps. Early pilot measurements with commercial sensors showed that skin conductance on the biceps was well below the amplitudes typical of palmar recordings, and the biceps is not an established \gls{eda} measurement site in the literature~\cite{boucsein_electrodermal_2012, gersak_electrodermal_2020}. The scope of the thesis was therefore adjusted. The validation is conducted on the palm, where \gls{eda} is well characterized, and the front-end can be verified against established signal morphology. A front-end that operates at the palm imposes no site-specific constraints on the electronics, so the results apply to any future biceps deployment. Biceps placement remains the clinical target and is discussed as future work.

% 7. Conclusion: the contribution
This thesis describes the design and validation of a custom analog front-end with galvanic isolation between the two signal chains for simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} acquisition, as a step toward objective stress and pain monitoring in non-verbal patients.


\section{State of the Art}
% Justify The Gap paragraph
To the author's knowledge, no previous study has attempted to measure \gls{eda} and \gls{emg} simultaneously from the same muscle site. Studies have combined \gls{eda} with \gls{ecg}~\cite{affanni_dual_2019, affanni_wireless_2020}, and one has combined \gls{eda} with \gls{ecg} and \gls{emg}~\cite{mcnaboe_design_2022}, but in all cases the \gls{eda} electrodes were placed at palmar or finger sites, separate from any \gls{emg} electrodes.

% 1. Multimodal wearable devices
% Both Affani papers: Shared electrode between EDA and ECG is feasible
% when the EDA modality is endosomatic
Acquiring multiple biosignals simultaneously improves the accuracy and specificity of stress and pain assessment~\cite{mcnaboe_design_2022}. \textcite{affanni_dual_2019} demonstrated that \gls{eda} and \gls{ecg} can be acquired from shared hand electrodes by adopting an endosomatic measurement approach, in which no external current is injected, and the skin potential is measured passively. Since no excitation current is injected, the \gls{eda} channel introduces no interference into the co-located \gls{ecg} channel. However, endosomatic \gls{eda} measures skin potential rather than skin conductance and does not provide the conductance signal required by the present design. This approach, therefore, does not extend to exosomatic \gls{eda}, in which an external source drives current through the skin to measure conductance. \textcite{mcnaboe_design_2022} presented a wearable device for the simultaneous acquisition of \gls{eda}, \gls{ecg}, and \gls{emg}. In that design, \gls{eda} was measured from the fingers and \gls{emg} from the biceps. Placing the sensors at separate sites avoids electrical interference, but the spatial correlation between muscle activity and electrodermal response is lost.

% 2. Co-located EMG and bioimpedance
Co-located \gls{emg} and bioimpedance measurement can be separated in the frequency domain by injecting an \gls{ac} carrier above the \gls{emg} band~\cite{ngo_wearable_2022}. \gls{emg} energy is bounded below \SI{500}{\hertz}, while the bioimpedance carrier is placed in the kilohertz range, so the two signals occupy non-overlapping frequency bands and can be recovered by filtering. Frequency-domain separation does not apply to \gls{dc} \gls{eda}, because there is no carrier frequency to separate from the \gls{emg} signal.

% 3. Galvanic isolation for simultaneous bioimpedance measurements
\textcite{kusche_galvanically_2017} showed that galvanic isolation between current source ground domains eliminates channel coupling in multi-channel bioimpedance measurement. When multiple current sources share a common ground, part of each excitation current returns through unintended electrode paths, corrupting the voltage measurement at each channel. In that design, each current source was assigned a separate ground domain, so that each excitation current returned only through its intended electrode. Channel coupling remained below \SI{-48}{\decibel} across the operating frequency range. The authors applied \gls{ac} excitation in the kilohertz range and isolated channels at separate body sites, not a \gls{dc} \gls{eda} excitation current from a co-located \gls{emg} reference electrode.

% Closing paragraph: Each area has addressed part of the problem, but no
% existing work combines co-located EMG and EDA acquisition with galvanic
% isolation as the isolation strategy.
The three areas reviewed each resolve a different aspect of the interference problem, but none address the specific challenge of co-locating \gls{emg} and \gls{dc} \gls{eda} at the same muscle site. Endosomatic \gls{eda} measures skin potential rather than skin conductance and does not provide the \gls{dc} conductance signal required by the present design. Frequency-domain separation supports co-located \gls{ac} bioimpedance and \gls{emg} but cannot be applied when the excitation is \gls{dc}. Galvanic isolation eliminates ground-loop corruption between channels but has not been extended to simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{dc} \gls{eda} acquisition at the same site. This combination defines the gap addressed by the thesis.

\section{Objectives}
% State the research question first. Then list the specific objectives
% Map into the validation questions
This thesis investigates how \gls{emg} and \gls{dc} \gls{eda} can be acquired simultaneously from the same muscle site using a custom analog front-end with galvanic isolation between the two circuits.
The following objectives guide the work:

\begin{enumerate}
    % 1. Design and implement the front-end
    \item Design and implement a custom analog front-end for simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} acquisition from the same muscle site, with galvanic isolation between the two signal chains.
    % 2. Characterize each signal chain individually
    \item Characterize the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} front-ends individually, including signal quality, frequency response, and the effect of the isolation stage on each signal chain.
    % 3. Validate simultaneous operation
    \item Determine whether simultaneous operation of both front-ends introduces mutual interference between the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} measurements.
    % 4. Assess reproducibility
    \item Assess the reproducibility of simultaneous \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} measurements across repeated acquisition sessions.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Thesis Outline}
This thesis is divided into six chapters.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chapter 2 covers the physiological basis of \gls{emg} and \gls{eda}, the measurement techniques for each signal, and the ground loop problem that arises from co-locating the two measurement circuits.

    \item Chapter 3 describes the hardware design of the analog front-end, including the \gls{emg} and \gls{eda} signal chains, the galvanic isolation stage, and the power supply. It also covers the validation protocol, data acquisition setup, and the signal-processing pipeline used for validation.

    \item Chapter 4 presents the results of the validation experiments.

    \item Chapter 5 discusses the design choices and the observed results in the context of existing work.

    \item Chapter 6 states the contribution of the work, discusses its significance, and identifies open problems for future work.
\end{itemize}
